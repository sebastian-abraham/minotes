2. Concepts#

2.1 Hazard and risk#

In plain English. A hazard is a live wire lying on the floor. The wire is dangerous whether or not anyone is in the room. Risk is the chance that somebody touches it before lunch. Standing further back does not make the wire safe. It makes the risk small.

The detail. - Hazard — a source with the potential to cause injury or ill health (ISO 45001 wording). - Risk — the combination of how likely harm is and how bad it would be. - Risk therefore needs exposure. Remove the exposure and the risk falls. - The hazard stays exactly where it was. That is the sentence this topic turns on. - Residual risk — what remains after controls; the number a safety officer signs off. - Risk assessment runs in four moves: identify, analyse, evaluate, treat. - Individual risk is one person's chance of harm. Societal risk is the chance of many people harmed in one event. - The F–N curve plots frequency against the number of fatalities (standard). - ALARP means the risk has been cut as low as is reasonably practicable (standard). - A 5 × 5 matrix scores likelihood against severity and lands in low, medium, high or extreme (standard).

Question Hazard Risk
What is it? A source of harm The chance of harm from that source
Can it be removed? Only by removing or replacing the agent Yes, by any control at all
Depends on The material, the energy, the design Exposure, duration, frequency, safeguards
Reduced by PPE? No Yes
Example Petrol in a tank Welding beside that tank

What to write (3 marks). Two definitions, one line of contrast, one consequence. Say that the hazard is inherent and the risk is conditional. Close with the point that substitution kills the hazard while PPE only lowers the risk.

Mark anchor. "The hazard is inherent; the risk is conditional." A script with that sentence has understood the pair rather than memorised two definitions.

Exam trap. Do not write "eliminate the risk" when you mean "eliminate the hazard". A risk is a probability and cannot be eliminated. It can only be reduced, and the leftover is residual risk.

2.2 Types of hazards#

In plain English. Factories hurt people in a small number of recognisable ways. Someone is struck, crushed, burned, poisoned, infected, deafened, or worn down slowly. Sorting hazards into families is how an inspector knows what to look for first.

The detail. - The standard split is seven families: physical, mechanical, electrical, chemical, biological, ergonomic and psychosocial. - Fire and explosion are usually carried as a separate family in process plants. - Every family has a signature harm. The signature is what the mark scheme wants beside the name. - Hazards also split by energy source: gravity, motion, electricity, pressure, heat, chemical reaction, radiation (standard). - They split again by mode of contact: struck-by, caught-in, contact, inhalation, ingestion, absorption (standard). - In a process plant the chemical family dominates, because the inventory is large and the harm is delayed. - In construction the mechanical and physical families dominate, because the work is at height and in motion.

Family Examples Signature harm
Physical Noise, vibration, heat, cold, radiation, pressure Deafness, heat stress, burns
Mechanical Unguarded shafts, moving parts, falling loads Cut, crush, amputation, fracture
Electrical Live parts, arc flash, static discharge Shock, burns, ignition
Chemical Solvents, acids, dusts, gases, fumes Burns, poisoning, fire, sensitisation
Biological Bacteria, viruses, moulds, insects Infection, allergy
Ergonomic Repetition, posture, manual handling Musculoskeletal disorder
Psychosocial Shift work, workload, harassment Stress, fatigue, error
Fire and explosion Flammable inventory, dust clouds Burns, blast, collapse

What to write (3 marks). Name the families in one line each, with one example and one harm per family. A table is faster than prose and scores the same.

Mark anchor. Seven families with a signature harm each. The harm is worth more than the example, because it proves you know why the family exists.

Exam trap. "Physical" and "mechanical" are not interchangeable. Noise and heat are physical. A rotating shaft is mechanical. Putting noise under mechanical signals a list copied without understanding.

2.3 Fire — the tetrahedron, the five classes, and what escapes#

In plain English. Fire needs four things at once: something to burn, air, heat, and a runaway chain reaction. Take any one away and the fire stops. Every extinguisher in the catalogue is just a device for taking one of those four away quickly.

The detail. - The fire triangle has three sides: fuel, oxygen, heat. - The fire tetrahedron adds a fourth: the self-sustaining chain reaction. - That fourth side is why dry chemical powder works on a fire it cannot cool or smother well. - Four extinguishing methods map onto the four sides: starvation, smothering, cooling, chemical inhibition. - Flash point — the lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off enough vapour to ignite. - Fire point — the slightly higher temperature at which the vapour keeps burning. - Autoignition temperature — the temperature at which a material ignites with no spark. - Fire classes follow the Indian and European convention, running A to E (slide 18).

Class Fuel Everyday examples Extinguish by
A Solid combustibles Wood, paper, cloth, rubber, plastics Cooling — water, foam
B Flammable liquids Petrol, kerosene, diesel, paint, solvents Smothering — foam, CO₂, powder
C Flammable gases LPG, acetylene, hydrogen, methane Shut the supply off, then cool
D Combustible metals Sodium, potassium, magnesium, aluminium powder Dry powder for metal, dry sand
E Live electrical equipment Panels, motors, wiring, switchgear Isolate, then CO₂ or clean agent

What escapes. A fire is only one of three outcomes from the same inventory. - Pool fire — a burning puddle of liquid, radiating heat onto its neighbours. - Jet fire — pressurised gas burning as a torch flame from a failed flange. - Flash fire — a vapour cloud ignites and burns back through it in seconds. - Vapour cloud explosion — a confined or congested cloud deflagrates and produces a blast. - BLEVE — a vessel heated until the liquid boils and the shell bursts (standard). - Dust explosion — a suspended dust cloud ignites; the first blast shakes loose more dust. - Toxic gas release — the fire itself makes the poison: carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide, hydrogen chloride, sulphur dioxide (standard).

What to write (3 marks, Q90). The five classes with fuel and one example each. For Q9, add the three escape routes: fire, explosion, toxic release.

Mark anchor. Five classes, and in the Indian scheme C is gas and E is electrical. Add that E fires are attacked only after isolation.

Exam trap. The American NFPA scheme uses A, B, C, D with C for electrical and a separate K for cooking oil. Writing NFPA letters under an Indian question loses the classification mark.

2.4 Extinguishers and their selection#

In plain English. An extinguisher is a pressure bottle of something that removes one side of the fire tetrahedron. Pick the wrong bottle and you either do nothing or make it worse. Water on burning petrol spreads the fire. Water on a live panel electrocutes the user.

The detail. - Definition — a portable, pressurised first-aid device that discharges an agent onto a small fire (slide 18, slide 19). - Water is the primary agent, sometimes with wetting agents added (slide 18). - Water freezes, so unheated sites need antifreeze or a different agent (slide 18). - Water mist sprays distilled water as a fine fog, for rooms where residue ruins equipment. - The deck names operating rooms, museums and book collections as water-mist sites (slide 18). - CO₂ leaves no residue, so it suits delicate and costly electronics (slide 19). - The deck lists CO₂ for Class B and Class C fires (slide 19). - CO₂ fails outdoors in wind, because the gas blows away before it smothers (slide 19). - CO₂ in a confined space displaces oxygen and needs caution (slide 19). - Foam blankets a liquid surface and seals the vapour. - Dry chemical powder interrupts the chain reaction; ABC powder covers A, B, C and E. - Wet chemical saponifies hot cooking oil, the class F or K fire (standard). - Clean agents such as FM-200 leave no residue and are safe in occupied rooms (standard). - Dry sand or graphite is used on metal fires, where water reacts violently (standard).

Agent Classes Works by Never use on
Water A Cooling Live electrical, burning liquids, metals
Foam A, B Smothering and sealing Live electrical
CO₂ B, C, E Smothering, some cooling Confined spaces without caution
Dry powder ABC A, B, C, E Chain inhibition Delicate electronics (residue)
Wet chemical F (cooking oil) Saponification Most other classes
Clean agent A, B, C, E Smothering and inhibition Cost-critical sites only

Choosing and siting. Selection runs class first, agent second, size third, position last. - The extinguisher must match the class of fuel present in that room. - Siting follows IS 2190: one water-type unit per 100 m², travel distance under 15 m (standard). - Mount the unit on a bracket with the handle about 1.5 m from the floor (standard). - Keep the unit visible, signed, and clear of stock and forklift routes. - Check the pressure gauge and the service tag monthly, and refill after any use. - A freezing or corroding location needs a different agent or a cabinet. - Staff must be trained to use it, or the unit is furniture.

What to write (3 marks, Q10 and Q11). Define the extinguisher in one line. Then list the pre-installation checks: class match, right number for the area, unobstructed siting, temperature, service date, signage, training.

Mark anchor. CO₂ for electrical equipment and for Class B, because it leaves no residue. The residue point is the reason, and the reason is the mark.

Exam trap. Water is not "the general extinguisher". On Class B it spreads the fuel. On Class E it conducts. On Class D it can react and explode. Water is a Class A answer only.

2.5 Inventory analysis and hazard rating#

In plain English. The size of a fire is set by how much fuel is sitting there, not by what lit it. So the first question in a process plant is boring and powerful: what is on site, where is it, and how much of it is there at once?

The detail. - Inventory — the mass of hazardous material held in a unit, a tank, or a pipeline at any moment. - Inventory analysis lists every hazardous material, its quantity, its location and its properties. - The aim is a fire and explosion hazard rating for each part of the plant. - Hold-up inside pipework counts. A long line holds more than the vessel it feeds. - Capital density — the value of equipment packed into a given area. Dense plants lose more per event. - The Dow method uses the spill of a flammable liquid 3 inches deep as its reference event (slide 48). - NFPA 704 rates a material from 0 to 4 on health, flammability and instability (standard). - The diamond reads blue for health, red for flammability, yellow for instability, white for special hazards (standard). - Mond extends Dow by adding a toxicity index (standard). - Indian law sets threshold quantities in the MSIHC Rules, 1989, Schedules 1 to 3 (standard). - Above the threshold, the site must prepare an on-site emergency plan and notify the authority.

Rating tool What it scores Scale
NFPA 704 One material's health, fire, reactivity 0–4 per quadrant
Dow F&EI One process unit's fire and explosion exposure 1 to 159+
Mond Dow plus toxicity Index plus toxicity rating
MSIHC threshold Whether the site is a major accident hazard site Quantity against schedule

Why inventory matters more than ignition. Ignition sources are countless and cheap to control one at a time. Inventory is one number, and it can be cut deliberately. - Buy in smaller lots and hold less in the plant. - Remove intermediate storage tanks between process steps. - Shorten pipe runs and reduce line diameter where hold-up is large. - Move storage away from process units to increase separation. - A smaller inventory makes every downstream number smaller: fire size, exposure radius, loss.

What to write (5 marks). Define inventory, list what is counted, name the rating tools, and close on inventory reduction as the cheapest real control.

Mark anchor. The 3-inch spill as Dow's reference event, and capital density as one of the five screening criteria for choosing a unit.

Exam trap. Inventory analysis is not a stock audit. The output is a hazard rating per unit, not a stores list. A script that counts drums but never rates a unit has answered the wrong question.

2.6 The Dow Fire and Explosion Index — what it is#

In plain English. The Dow Index turns a process unit into a single number that says how much fire and explosion damage it could do. You fill in a form, add up penalties, multiply, and read a score. It began because the chemical industry's loss record in the 1960s was bad enough to demand a scoreboard.

The detail. - The method originated in 1966, from a poor accident record in the chemical industry (slide 26). - It was one of the first hazard analysis systems built for chemical plants (slide 26). - Its selling point is that it puts damage in money and days, not adjectives (slide 26). - The deck's own description of the paperwork is that you fill it out like a tax form (slide 26). - Three verbs: quantify the damage, identify the equipment that can escalate, and communicate the potential to designers and operators (slide 28). - The method needs engineering judgement and is not perfect (slide 26, slide 27). - It is still upgraded and still used (slide 26).

Five items are needed to build one (slide 33) — this is the answer to Q12 and Q42. 1. An accurate plot plan, for an existing plant. 2. A preliminary plot plan, for a new plant. 3. A process flow sheet. 4. The F&EI hazard classification guide. 5. The F&EI form itself.

Choosing the unit. Not every vessel is analysed (slide 34). Screen on five things: - The chemical energy potential, which becomes the material factor. - The quantity of hazardous material in the unit. - The capital density of the area. - The process pressure and temperature. - The past history of problems in that unit.

The four numbers. The index is a chain of multiplications (slide 29, slide 45). - Material Factor (MF) measures flammability and reactivity, adjusted for flash point and boiling point (slide 35). - F1, general process hazards, set the magnitude of a loss (slide 40). - F2, special process hazards, set the probability of a loss (slide 41). - Unit hazard factor F3 = F1 × F2, and F&EI = MF × F3 (slide 45).

F1 — general process hazards F2 — special process hazards
Exothermic or endothermic reaction Toxic material, at 0.2 × NH
Material handling and transfer Sub-atmospheric pressure, 0.5
Enclosed or indoor process unit Dust explosion
Access Operating in the flammable range
Drainage and spill control Low-temperature operation
Corrosion and erosion
Leakage at joints and packing
Rotating equipment
Pressure penalty
Quantity of flammable material
Fired equipment

Loss control credits are features that prevent a serious incident or shrink it, grouped as process control, material isolation and fire protection (slide 30).

Reading the score (slide 47).

F&EI Degree of hazard
1–60 Light
61–96 Moderate
97–127 Intermediate
128–158 Heavy
159+ Severe

What to write (5 marks, Q93 and Q169). Definition and origin in 1966. Then the three verbs, the MF × F1 × F2 structure, the hazard bands, and one line on the credits.

Mark anchor. 128 is the trigger for a more detailed PHA (slide 47). That single number is the most useful fact in the topic, because it links the index to the next chapter.

Exam trap. MF is not the NFPA rating. MF is a number from about 1 to 40 built from the flammability rating NF and the reactivity rating NR, then adjusted. Quoting "MF = 3" from a 0-to-4 scale is the commonest error in the whole module.

2.7 The Dow calculation, step by step#

In plain English. The calculation is a recipe with fifteen steps and no cleverness in it. Each step produces a number that the next step needs. What earns marks is the order, and knowing what each number means when you arrive at it.

The detail. The chain is fixed and runs in this order. 1. Select the pertinent process unit and state why. 2. Assign the material factor MF from the classification guide. 3. Add the F1 penalties for the general process hazards. 4. Add the F2 penalties for the special process hazards. 5. Compute F3 = F1 × F2. 6. Compute F&EI = MF × F3 and read the hazard band. 7. Take the exposure radius from the empirical curve, then compute the area. 8. Treat the volume of exposure as a cylinder with height equal to radius (slide 49). 9. Value the equipment inside that volume at replacement cost. 10. Read the damage factor from the F3 and MF curves (slide 50). 11. Base MPPD = value of exposure × damage factor (slide 51). 12. Actual MPPD = base MPPD × credit factor (slide 53). 13. Read MPDO from the actual MPPD curve (slide 54). 14. BI = MPDO × (lost profit per day + fixed cost per day) (slide 55). 15. Act: cut the hazard first, add credits second, increase spacing third (slide 57).

Worked example. A solvent recovery unit, illustrative numbers throughout. - Material factor MF = 16, a toluene-class flammable solvent. - F1 penalties: exothermic 0.30, handling 0.50, enclosed 0.25, access 0.20, drainage 0.25. - F1 = 1.00 + 1.50 = 2.50. - F2 penalties: toxic 0.40, flammable range 0.50, corrosion 0.20, leakage 0.30, rotating 0.50, pressure 0.30, quantity 0.40. - F2 = 1.00 + 2.60 = 3.60. - F3 = 2.50 × 3.60 = 9.00. - F&EI = 16 × 9.00 = 144, which is Heavy (128–158) and past the 128 trigger. - Equipment value inside the exposure area is taken as ₹40 crore. - Damage factor off the curve is 0.62, so base MPPD = 40 × 0.62 = ₹24.8 crore. - Credit factor 0.75, so actual MPPD = 24.8 × 0.75 = ₹18.6 crore. - MPDO is read as 60 days, and lost profit plus fixed cost is ₹12 lakh a day. - BI = 60 × 12 lakh = ₹7.2 crore, so the event costs about ₹25.8 crore in total.

What the result means. The number is not a prediction. It is a ranking and a budget. - It ranks this unit against every other unit on the same site. - It sets the exposure radius, which decides plant layout and separation. - It prices the loss in property damage and in days of production. - It tells you where a credit is worth buying and where it is waste. - The deck's own conclusion is that reducing the hazard early beats adding credits late (slide 57).

What to write (8 marks, Q94). The step list, then the worked chain, then three lines on what the answer means. Show the arithmetic. An examiner cannot award method marks for an unshown product.

Mark anchor. F3 = F1 × F2 and F&EI = MF × F3. Two multiplications carry the entire method. Write them as equations, not as sentences.

Exam trap. The exposure radius comes from an empirical curve, and the deck gives no equation (slide 48). Do not invent a formula with a constant in it. Say "from the empirical curve" and move on to the damage factor.

2.8 Preliminary Hazard Analysis#

In plain English. PHA is the first pass. Before there is a drawing, a small team sits down and asks what could go wrong with this idea. It is cheap because it happens before anything is built, and rough because nothing is designed yet.

The detail. - PHA is usually the first attempt in the system safety process (slide 59). - It is used in the early stages of system design (slide 59). - It is semi-quantitative: it ranks hazards rather than measuring them (slide 59). - It identifies potential hazards and the accidental events that could follow (slide 59). - It ranks those events by severity and names the controls and follow-up actions (slide 59). - It also travels under the names Rapid Risk Ranking and HAZID (slide 59). - It leans on brainstorming and expert judgement (slide 60). - One or two knowledgeable people usually carry it out (slide 60). - It applies to any activity or system, at any level (slide 60). - Its quality depends on documentation, leader training and team experience (slide 60).

The four steps (slide 62, slide 63). 1. Prerequisites — form the team, describe the system, collect risk information from similar systems. 2. Hazard identification — consider every part of the system and record every finding. The deck's rule is that no hazard is too insignificant to record (slide 62). 3. Consequence and frequency estimation — for each event, estimate how often and how bad. 4. Risk ranking and follow-up — place each event on a risk matrix and let the ranking set the action.

Advantages (slide 61): it helps ensure the system is safe; changes are cheaper and easier early in design; it shortens design time by reducing surprises. Disadvantages (slide 61): hazards must be foreseen by the analysts; interactions between hazards are not easily recognised.

Output. A hazard register with severity ranking and recommendations, produced before the P&ID exists.

What to write (3 marks, Q53). Definition, the early-design timing, the semi-quantitative nature, and the four steps in one line each. For Q54, swap the last item for the two advantages and two disadvantages.

Mark anchor. "No hazards are too insignificant to be recorded" (slide 62). Also worth a mark: PHA is also called HAZID and Rapid Risk Ranking.

Exam trap. PHA is not a small HAZOP. HAZOP examines deviations from a defined design intent, node by node. PHA has no design intent to deviate from yet, so it works from experience and imagination instead.

2.9 HAZOP#

In plain English. HAZOP assumes the plant is designed correctly. Then it asks a rude question about every pipe: what if this flow is more, or less, or backwards? The team walks the plant line by line with a fixed list of rude questions, so nothing depends on somebody having a bright idea that afternoon.

The detail. - HAZOP is a structured and systematic technique for system examination and risk management (slide 64). - It finds hazards and also operability problems that would spoil the product (slide 64). - Its founding assumption: risk events come from deviations from design intent (slide 64). - Deviations are generated with guide words, the method's unique feature (slide 64). - A node is a section of the plant with stated boundaries and one stated design intent. - The node is normally bounded by isolation points such as valves or flanges. - A deviation is one guide word applied to one parameter. The pair is the unit of work.

Guide word Meaning Example with flow
NO or NOT None of the intent is achieved No flow through the line
MORE Quantitative increase Higher flow than design
LESS Quantitative decrease Lower flow than design
AS WELL AS Intent plus an extra component Solvent plus water ingress
PART OF Only some of the intent Only one phase arrives
REVERSE The opposite of the intent Backflow into the feed tank
OTHER THAN Something entirely different Wrong chemical transferred
  • Parameters are the second axis: flow, pressure, temperature, level, composition, time, pH, sequence.
  • Batch plant HAZOP adds early, late, before and after as guide words (standard).
  • Not every cell of the matrix is credible. The deck says so explicitly (slide 70).
  • Non-credible combinations do not need to be documented (slide 70).
  • Four phases carry the study (slide 65).
  1. Definition — pick a cross-functional team of subject specialists, fix the scope, boundaries, interfaces and assumptions (slide 66).
  2. Preparation — gather data, identify the audience, schedule meetings, agree the recording template and the guide-word set (slide 67).
  3. Examination — split the system into elements or steps, then apply the guide words to each element (slide 69, slide 70).
  4. Documentation and follow-up — record on the IEC 61882 template; the deck names that standard (slide 71).
  • Documentation then feeds risk review and risk communication (slide 72, slide 73).
  • The team climate matters: positive thinking and frank discussion are named requirements (slide 66).

A worked node. Node one: the feed line from tank T-1 to reactor R-1. Intent: transfer 5 m³/h of solvent at 2 bar and 30 °C.

Guide word + parameter Deviation Cause Consequence Safeguard
NO + flow Pump stops Power loss, pump failure Reactor starves, batch spoils Low-flow alarm, standby pump
MORE + flow Over-transfer Valve stuck open, controller fail Vessel overfills, spill, fire High-level trip, bund
LESS + flow Restricted flow Blockage, cavitation Poor mixing, hot spot Flow indicator, strainer
MORE + temperature Overheat Cooling failure, runaway reaction Pressure rise, relief lifts High-temperature trip
AS WELL AS + composition Water ingress Leaking jacket Corrosion, off-spec batch Conductivity check, drain
REVERSE + flow Backflow Check valve fails Contamination of feed tank Non-return valve, interlock
OTHER THAN + composition Wrong solvent Coupling error at unloading Wrong reaction, possible runaway Keyed couplings, labelling

What results (Q65). The study produces a document, not an opinion. - A deviation table: node, guide word, cause, consequence, existing safeguard, risk rank. - A recommendation for each unacceptable risk, with an owner and a date. - A ranked action list, which is what management actually reads. - Input to management of change, inspection plans, operating procedures and training. - Evidence for the regulator that the risks were examined systematically.

What to write (8 marks, Q128). Theory in one line, then the four phases. Then the seven guide words, the matrix against parameters, one worked deviation, and the output table.

Mark anchor. Seven guide words, and deviation = guide word × parameter. Add IEC 61882 as the documentation standard; it shows the method is a standard, not a habit.

Exam trap. "MORE" on its own is not a deviation. "MORE pressure" is. A script that lists guide words without pairing each to a parameter has listed vocabulary, not a method.

2.10 Choosing between PHA and HAZOP#

In plain English. PHA is a wide, shallow sweep early. HAZOP is a narrow, deep sweep late. Neither replaces the other, and the exam question is almost always about which to reach for.

The detail.

Point PHA HAZOP
Timing Concept and early design Detailed design and modification
Input needed System description, experience P&ID, design intent, node boundaries
Team One or two knowledgeable people Cross-functional specialists
Method Brainstorm against a hazard list Guide words applied to parameters
Output Ranked hazard register Deviation table with causes and safeguards

  • Use PHA when the design is still fluid and changes are cheap (slide 61).
  • Use HAZOP when the P&ID is fixed and a deviation has real consequences (slide 64).

Industry examples. A new paint shop gets a PHA at layout stage. A solvent recovery column gets a HAZOP on its P&ID. A warehouse of packaged goods gets a PHA and a fire risk assessment. A pharma reactor gets a HAZOP, then a LOPA on the exothermic reaction.

Mark anchor. PHA is wide and shallow and early; HAZOP is narrow and deep and late. The sentence is the answer to the whole comparison.

Exam trap. Do not claim HAZOP is "more accurate". HAZOP is more detailed, and detail is wasted on a design that will change next week. Timing, not accuracy, is the discriminator.

2.11 Chemical hazards and how they are classified#

In plain English. A chemical can hurt you in three separate ways. It can burn you, poison you, or catch fire. It can also damage the river rather than the worker. Classification systems exist so that the same symbol means the same thing on a drum in Kochi and a drum in Rotterdam.

The detail. - Physical hazards — flammable, explosive, oxidising, pyrophoric, self-reactive, water-reactive, compressed gas, organic peroxide (standard). - Health hazards — toxic, harmful, corrosive, irritant, sensitising, carcinogenic, mutagenic, toxic to reproduction (standard). - Environmental hazards — toxic to aquatic life, harmful to the ozone layer (standard). - Asphyxiants replace oxygen without being toxic themselves (standard). - The GHS is the Globally Harmonised System for classifying and labelling chemicals (standard). - GHS uses nine hazard pictograms, a red diamond border, and two signal words (standard). - The signal words are DANGER, for the more severe, and WARNING (standard). - Indian industry meets GHS through the MSIHC Rules, 1989 and the Factories Act (standard).

Pictogram Means Typical substance
Flame Flammable Petrol, acetone
Flame over circle Oxidiser Nitric acid, peroxides
Exploding bomb Explosive, self-reactive Ammonium nitrate
Gas cylinder Compressed gas LPG, oxygen
Corrosion Burns skin, attacks metal Sulphuric acid, caustic soda
Skull and crossbones Acute toxicity Cyanide salts
Health hazard Chronic disease, cancer Benzene, silica
Exclamation mark Irritant, harmful, sensitising Kerosene, resin
Environment Aquatic toxicity Chlorinated solvent

Routes of entry. A chemical hazard reaches the body by four roads, and the road decides the PPE (standard). - Inhalation — the fastest route in industry, because the lung has a huge surface and no barrier worth the name. - Skin absorption — solvents and pesticides pass through intact skin, so gloves matter as much as respirators. - Ingestion — from hands, food and cigarettes at the workbench. - Injection — from high-pressure leaks and sharps, rare but severe. - Target organs follow the chemical: solvents attack the nervous system and liver, metals the kidney and nerve, dusts the lung.

What to write (3 marks, Q57 and Q63). For Q57, the three groups with two examples each. For Q63, name the nine pictograms and give one substance per pictogram.

Mark anchor. Nine GHS pictograms and two signal words. The count is checkable, and an examiner can mark it in two seconds.

Exam trap. The skull and crossbones is acute toxicity. The "health hazard" pictogram with the chest is chronic harm, including cancer. Swapping them is a wrong answer, not a slip.

2.12 Controlling chemical hazards#

In plain English. The best way to stop a chemical reaching a worker is to not have the chemical. The second best is to keep it inside a box. Everything after that depends on people behaving correctly, which is why the order is not a matter of taste.

The detail. - Controlling exposure is the fundamental method of protecting workers (slide 84). - The deck uses a hierarchy of controls to choose between options (slide 84). - Elimination or substitution is the most desirable (slide 84). - Engineering controls come next, and must be the primary means (slide 84). - Administrative and work-practice controls suit cases engineering cannot reach (slide 84). - PPE is the least desirable but may still be effective (slide 84). - Respirators are required when engineering controls are infeasible, or while they are being installed (slide 84). - Engineering options: enclosure, closed transfer, local exhaust ventilation, general ventilation, scrubbers, gas detection, deluge (standard). - Administrative options: job rotation, exposure time limits, permits, training, housekeeping, health surveillance (standard). - Emergency options: spill kit, eye wash, safety shower, evacuation alarm, on-site plan.

Indian legal frame. Bhopal in 1984 produced Chapter IV-A of the Factories Act, inserted in 1987 (standard). - Section 41B requires the occupier to disclose information on the hazardous processes listed in the First Schedule (standard). - Section 41G requires a safety committee for factories in the hazardous list (standard). - The MSIHC Rules, 1989, cover manufacture, storage and import of hazardous chemicals (standard). - The Chemical Accidents Rules, 1996, require on-site and off-site emergency plans (standard). - The Factories Act also requires disclosure of information to workers (standard).

What to write (8 marks, Q58). Draw the hierarchy, then take three rungs and give one concrete measure for each. Close with the Indian statute that compels the control.

Mark anchor. "Engineering and work-practice controls must be the primary means; PPE is the least desirable but may still be effective" (slide 84). The second half stops you sounding as though PPE is forbidden.

Exam trap. PPE is not "the easy option that works". It protects one wearer, only while it fits and is worn correctly, and it fails silently. A leaking respirator seal looks exactly like a good one.

2.13 Hazardous properties of chemicals#

In plain English. A safety data sheet answers a short list of physical questions. How easily does it catch fire? How heavy is the vapour? How low can the air be before it will not burn? The answers decide where you store it and what you do when it leaks.

The detail. The deck's properties slide is a heading with no table under it (slide 86), so every value below is (standard). - Flash point — lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off ignitable vapour. - Fire point — the temperature at which the vapour keeps burning once lit. - Autoignition temperature — the temperature at which it ignites without a spark. - Vapour density — weight of the vapour relative to air. Above 1, it sinks. - Vapour pressure — how readily a liquid evaporates at a given temperature. - Boiling point — sets how fast a spilled liquid produces vapour. - LFL and UFL — the lean and rich limits of flammability in air. - Specific gravity — decides whether a spilled liquid floats on water. - pH — below 2 or above 12.5 signals a corrosive (standard). - LD50 — the dose killing 50 per cent of test animals, the standard acute toxicity figure. - Dust explosivity — measured as Kst, which sizes a dust explosion hazard (standard).

Petrol −43 °C About 3 to 4 1.4 to 7.6 per cent
Kerosene 38 to 74 °C About 5 0.7 to 5 per cent
Diesel 52 to 96 °C About 5 0.6 to 6.5 per cent
Methane Gas 0.55, rises 5 to 15 per cent
Hydrogen Gas 0.07, rises fast 4 to 75 per cent
Carbon monoxide Gas 0.97, mixes 12.5 to 74 per cent

Indian classification of petroleum follows flash point (standard). - Class A — flash point below 23 °C. Petrol and solvents. - Class B — flash point from 23 °C to 65 °C. Kerosene and high-speed diesel. - Class C — flash point from 65 °C to 93 °C. Heavy fuel oils.

How the three headline properties hurt the environment. - Toxicity kills beyond the fence. A release reaches water, soil and the food chain. - Flammability turns a spill into a fire, and the smoke carries the toxin downwind. - Reactivity makes the release unpredictable: heat, gas, or a runaway in a stored drum. - A substance that is toxic and flammable gives the responder no safe approach. - A substance that reacts with water cannot be fought with the obvious extinguisher.

What to write (5 marks, Q59). Define the three properties, give one number for each, then one consequence each for worker, plant and environment. Close on the combined case.

Mark anchor. Flash point below 23 °C is Class A petroleum; the bands break at 23, 65 and 93 °C. Indian-specific numbers beat generic ones in an Indian paper.

Exam trap. Flash point and autoignition temperature are different numbers with different meanings. Petrol has a flash point well below zero and an autoignition temperature around 250 °C. Confusing them suggests you have memorised a word, not a property.

2.14 Material Safety Data Sheets#

In plain English. An MSDS is the instruction sheet that comes with a hazardous chemical. It tells the worker what the stuff does, how to handle it, what to wear, and what to do when it spills or catches fire. If it is missing, the risk assessment for that area is guesswork.

The detail. - Definition — a document supplied by the manufacturer that states the properties, hazards, safe handling, emergency action and disposal for a chemical product. - The modern format has 16 sections, fixed by GHS and ISO 11014 (standard). - The order matters, because a responder reads section 5 while the fire is burning.

Section Content
1 Identification of the substance and supplier
2 Hazard identification, including GHS pictograms and signal word
3 Composition and ingredient information
4 First-aid measures
5 Firefighting measures
6 Accidental release measures
7 Handling and storage
8 Exposure controls and personal protection
9 Physical and chemical properties
10 Stability and reactivity
11 Toxicological information
12 Ecological information
13 Disposal considerations
14 Transport information
15 Regulatory information
16 Other information, including revision date
  • Section 8 is the one supervisors actually use, because it names the PPE.
  • Section 9 carries flash point, boiling point and vapour pressure.
  • Section 2 must match the label on the drum exactly (standard).
  • The sheet must be accessible at the point of use, not locked in an office (standard).
  • It must be in a language the workers read (standard).
  • It must be current: check the revision date in section 16.
  • Training must cover the sheet, or its presence is decoration.

What to write (3 marks, Q56). Definition, purpose in one line, the 16-section structure named, and the accessibility and training requirement. For 5 marks, add the table of the sections a supervisor uses most.

Mark anchor. 16 sections, with section 8 for exposure controls and PPE and section 9 for physical properties. The count proves you have seen a real sheet.

Exam trap. An MSDS is not a label. The label carries six elements: identifier, pictograms, signal word, hazard statements, precautionary statements and supplier. The sheet is the long document behind the label.

3.1 Three-mark answers#

A — Hazard and risk (Q7). 1. Hazard — a source with the potential to cause injury or ill health. 2. Risk — the combination of the likelihood of harm and its severity. 3. Contrast — the hazard is inherent in the agent; the risk depends on exposure.

B — Types of hazards (Q8). 1. Physical — noise, heat, radiation, pressure; deafness, heat stress, burns. 2. Mechanical — unguarded machinery, falling loads; cut, crush, fracture. 3. Chemical — solvents, acids, dusts; burns, poisoning, fire. 4. Add — biological, ergonomic, psychosocial, and fire and explosion.

C — Types of fire hazards (Q9). 1. By fuel class — A solids, B liquids, C gases, D metals, E electrical. 2. By outcome — fire, explosion, toxic gas release from the same inventory. 3. By mechanism — pool fire, jet fire, flash fire, vapour cloud explosion, BLEVE.

D — What a fire extinguisher is (Q10). 1. Definition — a portable pressurised device that discharges an agent onto a small fire. 2. Principle — it removes one side of the fire tetrahedron. 3. Limit — first-aid only; it is not a substitute for fixed protection or evacuation.

Quantify — express damage as property loss in money and outage in days. 3. Aim — identify escalating equipment, compare layout options, and communicate risk.

U — Role of PHA in modern safety management (Q122a). 1. Early — it is the first structured look, taken while design changes are still cheap. 2. Ranking — it sorts hazards by severity so money goes to the worst first. 3. Record — it creates the hazard register that later studies build on.

W — Pulling and pushing (Q132). 1. Push, do not pull — pushing keeps the load visible and the spine in compression. 2. Height — hands between hip and shoulder, body weight doing the work. 3. Surface — good floor, good wheels, and no slope or threshold on the route.

6. Say this if grilled#

6.1 Sixteen lines to have ready#

  1. A hazard is inherent and a risk is conditional. Substitution removes the hazard. PPE only lowers the risk.
  2. Dow started in 1966, because the chemical industry's loss record was bad enough to need a scoreboard. Its output is money and days, not adjectives.
  3. F&EI = MF × F1 × F2. Two multiplications carry the method, so show them as equations.
  4. 128 is the number that matters most. It is the trigger for a detailed PHA.
  5. The exposure radius comes off an empirical curve built on a 3-inch spill. The deck gives no equation, so quote the curve rather than invent a constant.
  6. Reducing the hazard early beats adding loss control credits late. The deck says so on its last summary slide.
  7. PHA is wide, shallow and early. HAZOP is narrow, deep and late. Detail is wasted on a design that changes next week.
  8. Deviation equals guide word times parameter. MORE alone is a word; MORE pressure is a deviation.
  9. In the Indian scheme C is gas and E is electrical. The NFPA letters are a different scheme.
  10. CO₂ is the electrical answer because it leaves no residue. The residue is the reason.
  11. PPE is last because it protects one wearer, only while it fits, and it fails silently. A dead extraction hood is visible; a leaking respirator seal is not.
  12. An MSDS has sixteen sections. Section 8 is the one the supervisor actually reads.
  13. A PHA without a ranking is only a hazard list. The ranking is the step that turns it into a decision.
  14. MSIHC came after Bhopal, and Chapter IV-A of the Factories Act is the same response written into factory law. Both arrive in 1987 and 1989, so the dates are close for a reason.
  15. A fire plan follows the fuel, not the floor plan. A water unit in a switch room is a hazard, not a control.
  16. The Dow number is not a prediction. It is a ranking and a budget, and it is meant to be argued with. A unit scoring 144 says "look here first", not "this will burn on Tuesday".

Module 1

## Introduction to Occupational Health and Toxicology

Occupational health as a discipline, the diseases work causes, and the toxicology that explains them.

**What the syllabus asks**Occupational safety and health – socio-economic reasons; health and safety in various industries; occupational diseases (musculoskeletal, hearing, carcinogens, silicosis, asbestosis, pneumoconiosis); toxic materials, exposure limits, toxicological investigation, industrial hygiene, organisational arrangements.

### 1.1What occupational health and safety is

Occupational health and safety is the branch of public health that deals with the place where adults spend
the largest single block of their waking life, and its unit of concern is the working population rather than the patient.

Occupational safety and health is concerned with protecting the safety, health and welfare of people engaged in work or
employment, and the goal of an OSH programme is a safe and healthy work environment. The definition deliberately reaches
further: OSH also protects co-workers, family members, employers, customers and others who might be affected by the
workplace environment. That clause is what makes the subject public health rather than a private arrangement between
master and servant. A worker who carries lead dust home on his clothes exposes his children; a solvent released inside a
factory leaves through the stack and enters the air of the surrounding locality; a driver who falls asleep after a
sixteen-hour shift injures strangers on the road. The boundary of the workplace is not the factory wall.

The international reference definition is jointly owned. Since 1950 the International Labour Organization and the World
Health Organization have used a common definition of occupational health, adopted by the Joint ILO/WHO Committee on
Occupational Health at its first session in 1950 and revised at its twelfth session in 1995.

**Definition (ILO/WHO)**

[Occupational health](#gt-1-occupational-health) should aim at: the promotion and maintenance
of the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being of workers in all occupations; the prevention among
workers of departures from health caused by their working conditions; the protection of workers in their employment from
risks resulting from factors adverse to health; the placing and maintenance of the worker in an occupational environment
adapted to his physiological and psychological capabilities; and, to summarise, the adaptation of work to man and of each
man to his job.

#### The five aims inside the definition

Each clause names a distinct activity, and an answer that lists them separately scores better than one that paraphrases
the whole sentence.

* **Promotion and maintenance** of physical, mental and social well-being — positive health, not merely the absence
  of diagnosed disease. This is why canteens, lighting, shift design and harassment policy sit inside OSH.
* **Prevention of departures from health** caused by working conditions — the classical core: stop the exposure
  before it produces the lesion.
* **Protection from risks** arising from factors adverse to health — where exposure cannot be removed, shield the
  worker from it.
* **Adaptation of the environment to the worker**, suited to his physiological and psychological capabilities — the
  argument for ergonomics and modified duties.
* **Adaptation of work to man and of each man to his job** — the placement function: match task demands to the
  individual, not the reverse.

The declared objectives follow from those aims: the maintenance and promotion of workers' health and working capacity; the
improvement of working conditions and the working environment so that they become conducive to safety and health; and the
development of work organisation and work cultures that reflect the value systems of the undertaking, including effective
managerial systems, personnel policy and principles for participation.

#### The moral, legal and financial case

OSH is justified on moral, legal and financial grounds, and the three arguments are used against different audiences.
The moral argument is protection of employees' lives and health, on the principle that no one should be injured or
shortened in life for the price of a wage. The legal argument rests on the preventive, punitive and compensatory effects
of law — statute obliges the employer to prevent harm, punishes failure and compensates the worker who is harmed. The
financial argument is that OSH reduces injury- and illness-related costs, including medical care, sick leave and
disability benefit costs.

**Duty of care**

Employers have a common law [duty of care](#gt-1-duty-of-care) — the duty to take reasonable
care of the safety of their employees. Statute law may impose general duties, introduce specific duties, and create
government bodies with powers to regulate occupational safety issues.

The common law duty is open-ended: it asks what a reasonable employer, knowing what was known at the time, would have
done, so it tightens as science improves. Statute law is specific and minimum — duties, exposure limits, inspectorates — so
compliance is a floor rather than proof of adequate care. An employer can be inside the statute and still negligent.

#### The worker, the work and the environment

Work matters socially and economically and plays a psychological role in developing self-esteem. The interaction
between a person and the physical and psychological working environment is continuous and two-way, and when that
interaction is associated with health hazards it ends in occupational disease. Risk is best held as a triad. The
**worker** brings age, sex, genetic susceptibility, nutrition, pre-existing disease, smoking, fitness and psychological
capability. The **work** brings task content, posture and force demands, pace, duration, shift pattern, repetition and
degree of control. The **environment** brings physical agents (noise, heat, cold, pressure, radiation, illumination),
chemical agents (dust, fume, vapour, gas), biological agents and the psychosocial climate. Risk is generated at the
intersection: the same silica dust is tolerable to a fit mason on a ventilated site and destructive to an asthmatic
sandblaster working twelve-hour shifts.

Responsibility for the triad sits with management, which frames and implements the systems and policies; a safe work
environment includes human relations, not only safe equipment. Particular emphasis belongs to women and children as the most
vulnerable groups, and to workers in dust, extreme heat and cold, poor light and loud sound.

**Exam pointer**"Enlist the objectives of occupational health" (3 marks) expects a list, not an
essay: the five aims of the ILO/WHO definition, or the three declared objectives. Give the 1950/1995 attribution in one
clause and move on.

### 1.2The socio-economic case

The numbers turn a moral argument into an economic one: work-related injury and disease are a measurable
drain on the employer, the household and the national product.

Globally, more than 2.78 million people die annually as a result of workplace-related accidents or diseases, one death
every fifteen seconds, and there are an additional 374 million non-fatal work-related injuries each year. The WHO estimate
is stated separately: almost 2 million people die each year attributable to exposure to occupational risk factors. The two
are not additive — they are different estimates on different definitions — and a script that adds them is wrong.

| Indicator | Figure quoted in the deck | What it counts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work-related deaths, global | More than 2.78 million a year, one every fifteen seconds | Fatal accidents plus fatal occupational disease |
| Non-fatal work-related injuries, global | 374 million a year | Reported injuries, all severities |
| Economic burden of work-related injury and death | Nearly 4 per cent of global GDP each year | Lost output, treatment, compensation |
| Cost of adverse occupational factors, national | 2–14 per cent of gross national product | Country estimates; the spread reflects informality and under-reporting |
| India, non-fatal occupational injuries | 17 million a year, 17 per cent of the world total | Reported non-fatal injuries |
| India, fatal occupational injuries | 45,000 a year, 45 per cent of world deaths from occupational injury | Fatal injuries |
| Occupational disease cases, world / India | 11 million / 1.9 million (17 per cent) | Diagnosed occupational disease |

The Indian share is worth memorising as a pair: roughly a sixth of the world's occupational injuries and disease cases,
but close to half its deaths from occupational injury. A high fatality ratio within a smaller injury count points at
severity — falls, collapse, entrapment and unguarded machinery in a largely informal construction and extraction workforce.
One slide also states "of 0.7 million deaths in the world, 0.12 (17 per cent) from India"; that is inconsistent with the
2.78 million global estimate quoted two slides earlier, so quote one set consistently.

#### What an injury costs the employer

The visible costs are medical care, sick leave and disability benefit, and the deck names exactly these three. The full
bill is longer, and its items are what an eight-mark answer should enumerate: direct costs of treatment, sick pay,
compensation and rising insurance premiums; the cost of recruiting and training a replacement, at lower productivity;
downtime, lost output and investigation; fines and legal costs; and the morale effect, since repeated accidents create fear
and an uncomfortable working environment and drive the best operators out.

The human cost of this adversity is enormous, and it is not carried by the balance sheet that caused it. The injured
worker loses income, skills and often the trade itself; the household loses an earner and gains a carer. Society absorbs
the treatment, the disability pension, the lost tax revenue and the premature death. This is the economic reason for
regulation: where the employer does not bear the full cost of the harm he creates he has no incentive to prevent it, and
the cost is externalised onto the worker, the family and the public purse. Statute law internalises it by imposing duties,
creating inspectorates with powers to regulate occupational safety issues, and shifting part of the residual burden onto
insurance and compensation.

An [occupational disease](#gt-1-occupational-disease) occurs primarily as a result of exposure to
risk factors arising from work activity, and counts as occupational when it is more prevalent in a given group of workers
than in the population at large. A [work-related disease](#gt-1-work-related-disease) has multiple
causes, in which work factors act together with other risks.

#### Why the figures understate the problem

Deaths from occupational disease are difficult to enumerate because of the time frame. Disease occurs in varying time
frames, from instantaneous development to gradual: instantaneous reactions follow chlorine or ammonia gas; a delay of some
six to twelve hours follows fumes of aerosolised zinc; a delay of weeks to months follows lead poisoning; a delay of
decades follows occupational carcinogens; and congenital malformations may appear in the children of parents exposed to
hazardous materials. A death certificate written forty years after the exposure rarely records the workplace, so the
attribution is lost — and work-related diseases with multiple causes, where work factors act together with other risk
factors, are systematically under-attributed compared with injuries, whose cause is visible on the day. Add the informal
sector, where neither the injury nor the employment is registered, and the recorded totals become a lower bound.

Good health and safety practice may also improve productivity rather than merely cost money, because human resources are
the most valuable resource an organisation holds.

* One work-related death every fifteen seconds; 374 million non-fatal injuries a year.
* Nearly 4 per cent of global GDP is lost each year to work-related injury and death.
* India carries about 17 per cent of the world's occupational injuries and disease but 45 per cent of its fatal
  occupational injuries.
* Cost falls in three places: the employer's bill, the household's lost income, and the state's health and welfare
  spending.
* Long latency is why occupational disease is under-counted, and why prevention must precede evidence of harm.

### 1.3Health and safety in major industries

Specific occupational safety and health concerns vary greatly by sector, because each industry produces a
characteristic agent, a characteristic route of exposure, and therefore a characteristic injury or disease pattern.

The deck's shortlist of the more dangerous industries is fishing, aviation, lumber, metalworking, agriculture, mining and
transportation. Danger concentrates where heavy plant, an uncontrolled physical environment (height, water, ground, weather)
and small mobile crews coincide.

#### Construction

Construction is one of the most dangerous occupations in the world, incurring more occupational fatalities than any
other sector. The signature event is the fall: falls are one of the most common causes of fatal and non-fatal injuries
among construction workers. The rest is a catalogue of the trade — work at height, excavation and collapse, scaffolding,
ladders, hoists and cranes, temporary electricity, moving plant, silica dust, hand-arm vibration, noise, heat, and manual
handling.

Control is unusually well codified here. The deck names the standard measures: proper safety equipment such as harnesses
and guardrails, and procedures such as securing ladders and inspecting scaffolding. Because accidents may have disastrous
consequences for employees as well as organisations, compliance with HSE construction requirements is treated as a
contractual as well as a legal matter, and the industry carries many rules and regulations for worker safety.

#### Agriculture

Agricultural workers are often at risk of work-related injuries, lung disease, noise-induced hearing loss, skin disease,
and certain cancers related to chemical use or prolonged sun exposure. On industrialised farms, injuries are caused by
machinery — tractors, power take-off shafts, harvesters, and the grain and silage stores that engulf workers in flowing
material. Pesticides can be hazardous to health, and workers exposed to them may experience illnesses or birth defects.

What distinguishes agriculture is the family as the workforce. Because families, including children, commonly work
alongside each other, agriculture is a common source of injuries among young workers, including fatal drowning, machinery and
motor vehicle accidents. No induction system reaches a child riding on a trailer, so control must be engineered into the
equipment and the layout.

#### The service sector

As service sector jobs have risen in developed countries, more and more jobs have become sedentary, presenting a
different array of health problems than manufacturing and the primary sector. Obesity, stress and overwork have further
complicated the interaction between work and health. The characteristic hazards are therefore musculoskeletal (sustained
posture at a screen, repetitive keyboard work, patient handling), psychosocial (workload, hours, emotional labour, violence
from clients) and indoor-environmental rather than traumatic.

#### Mining and oil and gas extraction

Workers in this industry have high prevalence rates of exposure to potentially harmful work organisation
characteristics and hazardous chemicals, and the deck quantifies it. Many worked long hours: 50 per cent worked more than
48 hours a week and 25 per cent more than 60 hours a week in 2010. Thirty-nine per cent had frequent skin contact with
chemicals; among non-smoking workers, 28 per cent had frequent exposure to second-hand smoke at work; and about two-thirds
were frequently exposed to vapours, gas, dust or fumes.

Those figures describe a distinct mechanism: exposure is not to one agent but to a mixture, in long shifts, in a remote
location, often on a rotating roster. Dust links the sector to pneumoconiosis, vapour and gas to acute neurological and
respiratory effects and to fire and explosion, the hours to fatigue-related accidents.

#### Manufacturing

Manufacturing concentrates the classical industrial exposures: unguarded machinery and nip points, noise from presses
and compressors, solvent vapour from degreasing and painting, metal fume from welding, dust from grinding and abrasive
blasting, heat from furnaces, and the manual handling and repetition of assembly work. Its injury pattern is dominated by
machine-related trauma and musculoskeletal disorder, its disease pattern by hearing loss, dermatitis, occupational asthma
and, over decades, the carcinogens of finishing and solvent work. Because the process is fixed and enclosed, this is also
the sector where engineering control works best: a guard or a local exhaust hood protects every worker on every shift
without depending on their behaviour.

| Industry | Characteristic hazard | Signature injury or disease | First-line control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Construction | Work at height, excavation, plant, silica dust | Fatal falls, struck-by, collapse; silicosis | Guardrails, harnesses, scaffold inspection, secured ladders |
| Agriculture | Machinery, pesticides, sun, animals, confined stores | Machine trauma, pesticide illness, skin cancer, hearing loss | Machine guarding, closed cab, PPE, re-entry intervals |
| Service sector | Static posture, repetition, workload, indoor air | Musculoskeletal disorder, stress and overwork, obesity | Workstation design, task rotation, workload management |
| Mining and oil/gas | Dust, vapours and gas, long shifts, skin contact | Pneumoconiosis, acute toxicity, fatigue-related accidents | Ventilation, dust suppression, roster limits, monitoring |
| Manufacturing | Unguarded machinery, noise, solvent and metal fume | Machine trauma, hearing loss, dermatitis, asthma | Machine guarding, enclosure, local exhaust ventilation |

#### The healthy workplace

**Definition (WHO)**

A healthy workplace is one in which workers and managers collaborate to use a continual improvement process to protect
and promote the health, safety and well-being of workers and the sustainability of the workplace, by considering, on the
basis of identified needs: health and safety concerns in the physical work environment; health, safety and well-being
concerns in the psychosocial work environment, including the organisation of work and workplace culture; personal health
resources in the workplace; and ways of participating in the community to improve the health of workers, their families
and other members of the community.

* Hazard follows the agent, the environment and the crew size, not the sector label.
* Construction's signature event is the fall; agriculture's is the machine and the family workforce.
* The service sector's burden is chronic and cumulative rather than traumatic.
* Mining and oil/gas combine mixture exposure with long hours: 50 per cent above 48 hours a week in 2010.
* A healthy workplace is defined by collaboration and continual improvement across physical, psychosocial, personal
  health and community domains.

### 1.4Musculoskeletal disorders

Musculoskeletal disorders are the commonest occupational ill-health problem in most economies precisely
because they are caused by ordinary work done in an ordinary way for an ordinary length of time.

**Definition**

[Musculoskeletal disorders](#gt-1-musculoskeletal-disorder) (MSDs) are conditions that affect
the muscles, bones and joints. The group includes tendinitis, carpal tunnel syndrome, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid
arthritis, fibromyalgia and bone fractures.

Two of those six must be separated in an answer. Osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and fibromyalgia are systemic or
degenerative conditions that exist independently of work; work may aggravate them but does not cause them. Tendinitis,
carpal tunnel syndrome and stress fracture have a clear occupational mechanism. The distinction matters because a diagnosis
of occupational MSD often carries compensation, and the test is whether the work exposure was a significant contributing
cause, not merely whether the worker happened to be at work.

MSDs are common, and risk increases with age because muscles, bones and joints deteriorate; the occupational component is
the part that can be designed out.

#### Sites, symptoms and causes

The symptoms are recurrent pain, stiff joints, swelling and dull aches. They can affect any major area of the
musculoskeletal system — neck, shoulders, wrists, back, hips, legs, knees and feet — and can interfere with everyday
activities such as walking or typing. The clinical signature of an occupational MSD is that it is worse at work, better at
weekends and on leave, and progressive over months; pain that is worst on waking, symmetrical, or accompanied by systemic
features points to inflammatory arthritis instead. Risk is affected by age, occupation, activity level, lifestyle and
family history, and the occupational part is carried by four activities named in the deck:

* **Sitting in the same position at a computer every day** — static loading of the cervical and lumbar spine and
  sustained contraction of the shoulder girdle, with reduced disc nutrition because movement drives diffusion.
* **Engaging in repetitive motions** — the same tendon sheath and muscle group loaded thousands of times without
  recovery, so microtrauma accumulates faster than repair.
* **Lifting heavy weights** — compressive and shear load on the lumbar discs, magnified by distance from the spine
  and by flexion and twisting.
* **Maintaining poor posture at work** — tissue held at the end of its range, where load is borne by ligaments
  rather than muscle.

Two named conditions carry much of the syllabus weight. **Manual handling** — lifting, lowering, pushing, pulling,
carrying — is the dominant cause of occupational low back pain, and what matters is not only the mass but the horizontal
distance from the spine, the height, the twist and the grip. [Repetitive
strain injury](#gt-1-repetitive-strain-injury) (RSI) is the umbrella term for upper-limb disorders produced by repeated forceful movement at speed:
tenosynovitis, epicondylitis and carpal tunnel syndrome, in which the median nerve is compressed by swollen flexor tendon
sheaths. Carpal tunnel syndrome is the cleanest example because the mechanism is mechanical and the exposure — prolonged
wrist flexion and extension with force, as in assembly, packing and keyboard work — is measurable.

Psychosocial factors are a co-factor, not an alternative explanation: high demand with low control, low supervisor
support, monotony, piece rates and fear of reporting pain all raise the probability that a physical load becomes a reported
disorder, because they remove the worker's ability to pace the task.

#### Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis begins with a physical examination checking for pain, redness, swelling, muscle weakness and atrophy, and
testing reflexes, since unusual reflexes may indicate nerve damage. Imaging — X-rays or MRI — examines bones and soft
tissues, and blood tests check for rheumatic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis. Treatment follows the diagnosis and its
severity: for occasional pain, moderate exercise and over-the-counter medication such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen; for
more severe symptoms, prescribed anti-inflammatory and analgesic medication; and in some cases physical therapy,
occupational therapy or both, which teach the worker to manage pain, maintain strength and range of motion, and adjust
daily activities and environments. Treatment is the wrong end of the problem: a disorder that has reached prescribed
medication has already cost more in lost productivity than the redesign would have.

#### Ergonomic prevention

| Level | Measure | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design of work | Mechanical aids, hoists, height-adjustable surfaces, material delivered at waist height | Removes the load instead of teaching the worker to bear it |
| Design of task | Job rotation, task enlargement, rest breaks, reduced repetition rate, no piece rates rewarding speed | Restores recovery time between loading cycles |
| Design of workstation | Screen at eye level, forearm support, neutral wrist, foot support, handle matched to grip | Keeps joints in mid-range, where load is muscular not ligamentous |
| Individual | Strengthening and stretching, tall posture, careful lifting, healthy lifestyle, weight control | Raises tissue tolerance and slows age-related decline |
| Organisational | Early reporting, modified duties, return-to-work planning, training in safe handling | Stops acute pain becoming chronic disability |

* MSDs affect muscle, bone and joint; the work-caused subset is tendinitis, carpal tunnel syndrome and repetitive
  strain injury.
* Four mechanisms: sustained static posture, repetition, heavy lifting, and posture at the end of range.
* Diagnosis is clinical first, with imaging and blood tests to exclude rheumatic disease.
* Manual handling is the main route to low back disorder; RSI the main route to upper-limb disorder.
* Ergonomic redesign outranks training, and training outranks treatment.

### 1.5Hearing impairment

Noise-induced hearing loss has the cleanest exposure–effect relationship of any occupational disease and the
worst compensation record, because the damage is permanent, painless and invisible until it is disabling.

**Definition**

Industrial deafness — also called industrial hearing loss or [occupational
hearing loss](#gt-1-noise-induced-hearing-loss) — is a form of hearing loss that occurs as a result of exposure to high levels of sound within the
workplace. It may present as temporary hearing loss, permanent hearing loss or tinnitus.

The condition most commonly affects those working in construction, transport (including truck drivers), music and
events, agriculture, manufacturing and mining — the loud trades and the merely chronically loud ones: a truck cab is not
painful, but eight hours a day of engine noise for twenty years deafens as reliably as a rock concert.

#### How the cochlea is damaged

Sound is transduced in the organ of Corti, where outer and inner hair cells sit on the basilar membrane inside the
cochlea. Each cell carries a bundle of stereocilia that shear against the tectorial membrane as the membrane moves,
opening mechanically gated channels and converting displacement into a nerve signal. The cells are metabolically expensive
and do not regenerate in mammals, so every unit lost is lost for life.

Two mechanisms destroy them. At high intensity the stereocilia are bent and fractured and the bundle is disorganised — a
mechanical injury. Simultaneously the cell is driven to fire continuously and its metabolic demand outruns its blood
supply, producing oxidative stress and cell death — a metabolic injury. The first cells to go are those tuned to the
highest frequencies at the base of the cochlea, which is why the earliest audiometric sign is a high-frequency notch and
the earliest symptom is difficulty hearing women's and children's voices rather than a loss of overall loudness.

#### Temporary and permanent threshold shift

After a few hours of loud exposure the hearing threshold rises and then recovers: a temporary threshold shift. It is a
real injury, not fatigue, and the deck's treatment advice follows from it — avoid loud sounds and let the hair cells rest,
and hearing should return within a day or so; if it has not returned within a couple of days, seek medical attention.
Repeated shifts with no time for recovery convert into a permanent threshold shift, in which hair cells and then the
associated nerve fibres are gone and the audiogram shows a permanent notch. The warning is the temporary shift, and it is
ignored because it resolves by itself.

Industrial deafness therefore develops from a noisy environment over a prolonged period without adequate hearing
protection, or from acoustic trauma — a one-off exposure to extremely high noise such as an explosion. A third route is
chemical: ototoxic agents such as toluene, styrene, carbon monoxide and some drugs damage the same hair cells, and combined
noise and solvent exposure is worse than either alone (standard reference; the deck treats noise as the sole cause).

#### Symptoms and treatment limits

The common signs are reduced hearing in one or both ears; difficulty understanding speech where there is background
noise; a constant ringing, whistling, hissing or buzzing; difficulty hearing the radio or television; missing portions of
conversations despite paying attention; and difficulty hearing higher-pitched sounds, including the voices of women and
children. The last two bring a worker to a clinic, because the loss falls in the speech frequencies used for consonant
discrimination: the patient hears that someone is speaking but cannot separate the words.
[Tinnitus](#gt-1-tinnitus) often accompanies the loss and is frequently the more distressing of
the two, because it is loudest in the silence that would otherwise be restful.

Treatment is limited, which is the strongest argument for prevention. No treatment is required for temporary loss beyond
rest and avoidance of loud sound; if the loss is permanent, hearing aids may be recommended, with needs assessed against the
worker's lifestyle. Where tinnitus is present, meditation, hearing aids and some lifestyle changes may help; tinnitus cannot
be cured. Nothing restores a dead hair cell.

#### Prevention

Unlike some other forms of hearing loss, industrial deafness can often be prevented. The deck's measures run from the
individual outward: hearing protection such as earmuffs and earplugs worn in loud environments; regular checking that
protection is in good working order and used appropriately; reducing the time exposed where possible; soundproofing and
noise reduction equipment; and regular hearing testing so that any change can be monitored. Because symptoms may not be
noticed for months or years, the measures must be used consistently.

The ordering should be reversed in an answer. Noise control at source — enclosure, silencers, damping, isolation of the
machine — removes the hazard for every worker permanently; administrative control reduces the dose by reducing time; hearing
protection is the last resort, because its real attenuation depends on fit, comfort and whether it is worn at all, and it
protects nobody else. Most jurisdictions set the action level at 85 dB(A) over an eight-hour day (standard reference; the
deck quotes no limit), and the equal-energy principle halves the permitted duration for every 3 dB rise above it.

* Outer hair cells transduce sound and never regenerate; those tuned to high frequencies die first.
* Temporary threshold shift recovers in a day or so; repeated shifts become permanent.
* Acoustic trauma is a single event; occupational loss is cumulative; ototoxic solvents add to noise.
* Tinnitus cannot be cured; hearing aids help the loss, not the ringing.
* Control at source first, time reduction second, hearing protection last, audiometry throughout.

### 1.6Occupational respiratory disease and carcinogens

Three dust diseases dominate this syllabus — silicosis, asbestosis and pneumoconiosis — and each is a
different mineral making the same kind of lung scar by a slightly different route.

**Definition**

[Silicosis](#gt-1-silicosis) is a fibrosing lung disease caused by inhaling respirable
crystalline silica, chiefly quartz. The particle is engulfed by an alveolar macrophage, which cannot digest it; the
macrophage dies and releases the silica again, so the inflammatory cycle repeats and collagen is laid down around the dust
foci.

The mechanism explains the two features an examiner looks for. First, size: only the respirable fraction — roughly below
5 µm, standard reference — bypasses the nose and the mucociliary escalator and reaches the alveoli, which is why the hazard
comes from cutting, grinding, drilling and blasting rather than from a heap of sand. Second, persistence: silica is neither
soluble nor metabolisable, so the macrophage's strategy of digesting and clearing the particle fails, and the failed attempt
is itself the injury. Released contents of the dying cell recruit further macrophages and stimulate fibroblasts, and the
result is a discrete, rounded hyaline nodule of concentric collagen, forming first in the upper lobes and coalescing into
progressive massive fibrosis. Hilar nodes may calcify in a rim, the "eggshell" pattern, and because silica impairs the
macrophage's ability to kill mycobacteria, silicosis carries an excess risk of tuberculosis.

Silicosis appears after years of exposure, is irreversible, and continues to progress after exposure stops, so prevention
is entirely environmental — wet methods, local exhaust ventilation, enclosure of the working area, substitution of a less
hazardous abrasive, and respiratory protection only as the last line.

**Definition**

[Asbestosis](#gt-1-asbestosis) is diffuse interstitial pulmonary fibrosis caused by inhaling
asbestos fibres, in which the retained fibre provokes chronic inflammation and collagen deposition in the alveolar walls
rather than discrete nodules.

Asbestos differs from silica in geometry, and the geometry is the pathology. Serpentine asbestos (chrysotile) forms curly
fibres; amphibole asbestos (crocidolite, amosite) forms straight, stiff, needle-like fibres that penetrate deep into the lung
and cannot be cleared or dissolved. The fibre is too long for a macrophage to engulf completely — frustrated phagocytosis —
so the cell spreads along it, releases oxidants and cytokines, dies, and leaves the fibre to the next cell. The result is
diffuse thickening of the alveolar interstitium, worst at the lung bases and subpleural regions, with fine crackles, finger
clubbing, progressive breathlessness, and pleural thickening and plaques on the radiograph.

Fibre type, dose and time since first exposure govern the risk, and latency is of the order of decades (standard
reference). Asbestosis is only one effect of the fibre: asbestos also causes pleural plaques and effusion, lung cancer (with
a multiplicative interaction with smoking), and mesothelioma of the pleura or peritoneum, almost uniquely attributable to
asbestos. Shipbreaking, insulation, roofing, brake linings and demolition are the classical exposures; at Alang,
shipbreaking is the standard Indian example. Control is by substitution — ban and replace — because no threshold has been
established for the carcinogenic effects.

**Definition**

[Pneumoconiosis](#gt-1-pneumoconiosis) is the general term for any lung disease produced by the
accumulation of inhaled mineral dust and the tissue reaction to it. Silicosis, asbestosis, coal workers' pneumoconiosis
and byssinosis are its named forms.

The most important member of the group after the two above is coal workers' pneumoconiosis. Coal dust is less fibrogenic
than silica, so the early lesion is a coal macule — a focal collection of dust-laden macrophages with a little reticulin,
centred on the respiratory bronchiole — with focal emphysema; with a sufficient dust burden the macules coalesce into
progressive massive fibrosis. Coal dust usually contains some silica, so the two diseases overlap in a real miner's lung;
byssinosis, from cotton and hemp dust, shows Monday-morning chest tightness that improves through the week.

| Disease | Agent | Typical exposure | Site and lesion | Distinctive feature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Silicosis | Crystalline silica (quartz) | Sandblasting, quarrying, stone cutting, foundry | Upper lobes; rounded hyaline nodules, coalescing to PMF | Eggshell hilar calcification; excess tuberculosis risk |
| Asbestosis | Asbestos fibres, amphiboles worst | Insulation, shipbreaking, roofing, brake linings | Lower lobes, subpleural; diffuse interstitial fibrosis | Pleural plaques, mesothelioma, lung cancer; long latency |
| Coal workers' pneumoconiosis | Coal mine dust | Underground coal mining | Upper lobes; coal macules with focal emphysema, then PMF | Dust macules rather than hyaline nodules |
| Byssinosis | Cotton, flax, hemp dust | Textile spinning and carding | Bronchi; airway obstruction, no fibrosis | Monday-morning chest tightness |

**Exam pointer**"Describe silicosis" and "Describe asbestosis" (3 marks each) want four things in
four sentences: the dust, the mechanism at the alveolus, the lesion with its site, and the prevention. The sentence that
earns the third mark distinguishes nodule (silica, upper lobes) from diffuse fibrosis (asbestos, lower lobes).

#### Carcinogens

A [carcinogen](#gt-1-carcinogen) is an agent capable of causing cancer in living tissue. Most
carcinogens produce chronic effects, appearing many months or years and usually decades after exposure, because cancer
requires a series of mutations in one cell lineage. Genotoxic carcinogens react with DNA and initiate the change, so no
threshold can be assumed and any exposure carries some risk; non-genotoxic agents promote division or suppress repair, and
for these a threshold may exist. The list runs from asbestos, silica and benzene to diesel exhaust, radon and chromium and
nickel compounds.

#### Metal fume fever

Metal fume fever is the acute, self-limiting illness caused by inhaling freshly formed metal oxide fume, most often from
zinc in galvanised steel welding, and also from copper, magnesium and brass. The deck's timing is the diagnostic clue: a
delay of some six to twelve hours between the end of the shift and the onset of symptoms. The worker develops fever, chills,
headache, myalgia, a metallic taste, dry cough and chest tightness, usually in the evening, and recovers within a day or two,
which is why it is dismissed as a chill. The mechanism is an acute inflammatory response of the alveolar epithelium to
freshly condensed oxide particles; repeated attacks produce tolerance rather than immunity. Control is extraction at the
arc, fume extraction guns, and a working position that keeps the plume out of the breathing zone.

* Below about 5 µm the particle reaches the alveolus; persistence, not solubility, drives the fibrosis.
* Silica: upper-lobe nodules, eggshell calcification, tuberculosis risk, no reversal after exposure stops.
* Asbestos: lower-lobe diffuse fibrosis plus plaques, lung cancer and mesothelioma; latency in decades.
* Pneumoconiosis is the umbrella term; the mineral decides the lesion.
* Metal fume fever appears six to twelve hours after welding galvanised steel and resolves in a day or two.

### 1.7Toxicology and exposure limits

Toxicology supplies the dose–response reasoning that says how much of an agent is too much, and exposure
limits turn that reasoning into a number a workplace can be measured against.

**Definition**

Industrial toxicology is the study of the harmful effects on humans of chemicals used in the workplace, the products
produced by companies, and the wastes created in manufacturing. Toxicology itself is the study of the adverse effects of
chemicals or physical agents on living organisms.

The common occupational toxic substances fall into four families. **Heavy metals** — lead, mercury, cadmium,
chromium — act on nerve, kidney and bone marrow and are retained for years. **Pesticides** — organophosphates,
carbamates, organochlorines — act on neurotransmission and cause the classic acute poisonings of agriculture.
**Organic solvents** — toluene, xylene, trichloroethylene — depress the central nervous system, defat the skin and, for
some, damage nerve or liver. **Gases, vapours and particulates** act according to solubility: ammonia attacks the upper
airway at once, phosgene reaches the alveoli.

Entry is by four routes. **Inhalation** dominates in industry: the lung offers an enormous surface, thin membranes
and a blood supply that carries the agent everywhere in seconds. **Skin absorption** matters for lipophilic solvents and
pesticides and is easily missed because there is no sensation. **Ingestion** follows hand-to-mouth contact, food eaten in
the work area and swallowed dust; **injection** occurs through a high-pressure jet or a contaminated sharp. Route decides
the control: a respirator does nothing for a solvent absorbed through the hands.

#### Dose, response and threshold

The founding principle is that the dose makes the poison: response depends on the amount reaching the target organ over
time, not on the substance's reputation. A **threshold** is the point at which toxicity first appears, and it occurs
where the body's ability to detoxify or repair toxic injury has been exceeded. The [LD50](#gt-1-ld50)
is the dose at which death occurs at 50 per cent response, the standard single number for comparing acute lethality, though
it says nothing about chronic effects, carcinogenicity or sensitisation.

Effects are classified on two axes. **Local** toxicity occurs at the site of contact — a caustic burn, solvent
dermatitis, a corneal injury. **Systemic** toxicity occurs distant from the point of contact and may involve many organ
systems, because the agent has been absorbed and distributed. **Acute** toxicity occurs almost immediately, within hours
or days, of an exposure; **chronic** toxicity is cumulative damage to specific organ systems, taking many months or years
to produce recognisable clinical disease, and most carcinogens produce chronic effects. The distinction matters because
acute effects are met by emergency planning and chronic ones only by long-term exposure control.

#### Exposure limits

**Definition**

The [Threshold Limit Value](#gt-1-threshold-limit-value) (TLV) is the average concentration of an
airborne substance to which most workers could be safely exposed over an eight-hour working day or forty-hour working week
throughout a working lifetime. The Maximal Allowable Concentration (MAC) is the peak or maximum concentration to which most
workers could be safely exposed.

| Limit | Averaging period | What it controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TLV–TWA | 8-hour day, 40-hour week | Chronic effects; the shift average, weighted for time spent in each task |
| STEL | 15 minutes (standard reference) | Acute effects from brief peaks; usually a multiple of the TWA (standard reference) |
| Ceiling | Instantaneous | Fast-acting irritants and asphyxiants where any excursion is dangerous |
| MAC | Peak | The deck's term for the maximum permissible peak concentration |

A TLV is a guideline for average healthy adult workers, not a line between safe and unsafe: susceptibility varies with
age, pregnancy and pre-existing disease, and the figure is meaningless without its averaging period.

Concentrations are expressed two ways. **ppm** (parts per million by volume) suits gases and vapours because it is
independent of molecular weight; **mg/m³** suits dusts, fumes and mists, because a particle has no meaningful volume
fraction. Conversion needs the molecular weight — mg/m³ = ppm × molecular weight ÷ 24.45 at 25 °C and one atmosphere
(standard reference) — so a limit in ppm cannot be compared with a dust sample in mg/m³ without arithmetic.

#### Biological monitoring and the toxicological investigation

Air sampling measures what is in the air; biological monitoring measures what has entered the body, by analysing blood,
urine, exhaled breath or hair for the agent or its metabolite — blood lead, urinary mandelic acid after styrene,
cholinesterase activity after organophosphate exposure. It integrates all routes of entry and all the protection actually
worn, which is its advantage and its difficulty: results depend on sampling time, on kidney and liver function, and on
non-occupational sources. It is used alongside air monitoring, never instead of it.

A toxicological investigation is the structured answer to the question of whether a substance is harming a workforce. It
establishes the agent and its toxicology from the safety data sheet and the literature; measures exposure by personal air
sampling and biological monitoring; reconstructs dose, including past and non-occupational exposure; looks for the health
outcome through clinical examination, surveillance, epidemiology and animal or in-vitro study; characterises risk against
the limit or against dose–response data; and specifies control.

#### Industrial hygiene

**Definition**

[Industrial hygiene](#gt-1-industrial-hygiene) is the art and science dedicated to the
anticipation, recognition, evaluation, communication and control of environmental parameters in, or arising from, the
workplace that may result in injury, illness, impairment, or affect the well-being of workers and members of the community.
Those parameters are divided into biological, chemical, physical, ergonomic and psycho-social categories.

The discipline works in three steps — identification of risks, evaluation by measurement against a standard, and control
by the appropriate hierarchy — and its physical hazards are noise, temperature and illumination extremes, ionizing and
non-ionizing radiation, and ergonomics, with indoor air quality as a related area.

* Four families of workplace toxicant: heavy metals, pesticides, organic solvents, gases and particulates.
* Inhalation is the dominant route; skin absorption is the one most often missed.
* Threshold, LD50 and dose–response are the three quantitative ideas behind every limit.
* A TLV–TWA is an 8-hour average; STEL and ceiling exist because averages hide peaks.
* Biological monitoring measures absorbed dose; air monitoring measures the source.

The deck's engineering measures occupy the middle of that ladder: designing the buildings, good housekeeping, general
ventilation, substitution, control of dusts by enclosure and isolation, local exhaust ventilation, protective devices,
environmental monitoring and research. Its medical measures are the surveillance layer — pre-placement examinations,
periodic examinations, medical and health care services, notifications, supervision of the working environment, maintenance
and analysis of records, and health education and counselling. Its legislative layer is statutory: policies and regulations
for factories and workplaces covering the health of workers, including insurance, sickness policies and disability benefits.

#### Why PPE comes last

PPE is the least preferred method because of the diligence required to ensure its effectiveness. It protects only the
person wearing it, so a respirator does nothing for the man beside him. Its performance depends on fit, on whether it is
worn for the whole exposure, and on storage and maintenance — a respirator left on a hook in a dusty store is a decorative
object. Failure is silent: a leaking seal gives no warning, whereas an extraction hood that has stopped is visible. And it
is uncomfortable, so it is removed exactly when the work becomes hardest, which is when exposure peaks. It remains essential
for short, unpredictable or emergency exposures, and while engineering control is installed.

#### The organisational machinery

What holds the hierarchy in place is managerial rather than technical. A **safety committee** with worker
representation gives the workforce a formal route to report hazards and obliges management to record and answer them.
**Training** must be specific to the task and repeated, because a control that is not understood is not used.
**Record keeping** — exposure measurements, medical examinations, incidents, maintenance of guarding and ventilation — is
what makes a trend visible and what defends the organisation when a claim arrives decades later. **Surveillance** closes
the loop: pre-placement examination sets a baseline and detects unfit placement, periodic examination detects early change
while it is still reversible, and notification feeds the statutory reporting system. **Audit and review** test whether
the arrangements still work after the plant, the process or the workforce has changed.

None of this functions without visible management commitment, because the hierarchy is a statement about where money is
spent. An organisation that buys respirators rather than an extraction hood has decided to transfer risk to the worker; one
that redesigns the task has decided to remove it. Statute law reinforces the engineering preference by imposing duties and
creating inspectorates, and the common law duty of care asks what a reasonable employer would have done — which, for a known
hazard with a known engineering control, is to install it.

Close with one sentence: the order is a hierarchy and PPE is last.

* Elimination, substitution, engineering, administrative, PPE — in that order, for a reason.
* Engineering control protects every worker without their cooperation; PPE protects one worker with it.
* Medical measures are surveillance, not prevention: they detect change after exposure has occurred.
* Records and notification make long-latency disease visible and compensable.
* Money reveals the real hierarchy: what the organisation buys first is what it believes in.

## Chemical and Biological Hazards, and Noise

What chemicals and microbes do to a body, and what noise does to an ear — with the controls for each.

**What the syllabus asks**Chemical hazards – dust, fumes, vapour, fog, gases – methods of control; biological hazards – classification of bio-hazardous agents, bacterial, viral, fungal and parasitic agents, infectious diseases, control at workplaces; noise – exposure regulation and control.

### 2.1Chemical hazards and their physical forms

A chemical hazard is defined by two things at once — what the substance does when it
reaches a target, and what physical form carries it there. In occupational hygiene the second usually
decides the first.

Hazardous substances are substances that can have a harmful effect on health, and the category is much
wider than the process industries suggest: paints, cleaners, disinfectants and glues contain hazardous
chemicals and are found in almost every home and workplace. What varies is the concentration, the
duration and the form in which the substance escapes.

#### Where the hazard comes from

Three origins are distinguished, because the control differs. **Naturally occurring** chemicals are in the material itself — mycotoxins from moulds in stored grain, pyrrolizidine alkaloids in certain herbs and fodder. **Intentionally added** chemicals serve a function: nitrite and sulfiting agents as preservatives. **Unintentionally or incidentally added** chemicals arrive as contamination — pesticide residues, lead, arsenic, mercury.

#### The six forms

Dust, fume, vapour, mist or fog, gas and fibre are not six classes of chemical — the same substance
can appear as several of them — but six classes of *airborne behaviour*. Each behaves differently
in air, is sampled by a different instrument, and is captured by a different control.

**Definition**

An [aerosol](#gt-2-aerosol) is a relatively stable suspension of solid
particles in air, of liquid droplets in air, or of solid particles dissolved or suspended in liquid
droplets in air. Dust, fume, mist and fog are all aerosols; a gas and a vapour are not.

**Definition**

A [fume](#gt-2-fume) is a cloud of solid particles formed by combustion,
sublimation or condensation of a vaporised material. The metal is vaporised by heat, then condenses in
cooler air into extremely fine oxide particles; nothing is ground or broken.

**Definition**

A [vapour](#gt-2-vapour) is the gaseous phase of a material that is ordinarily
solid or liquid at room temperature and pressure. A gas, by contrast, is already gaseous at room
temperature and pressure.

| Form | How it is generated | Behaviour in air | Typical site example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dust | Mechanical: grinding, milling, blasting, cutting, crushing, handling of powders | Solid particles; coarse fraction falls out within metres, fine fraction stays airborne for hours | Silica from concrete cutting and drilling; cement, wood and metal dust |
| Fume | Condensation of vaporised metal or of combustion products | Sub-micron solid particles; diffuses like a gas, does not settle readily, visible as a plume | Welding and soldering fume; lead fume from hot-cutting painted steel |
| Vapour | Evaporation from a liquid surface, or sublimation of a solid | Molecules mixed in air; commonly heavier than air and pools in pits, sumps and trenches | Solvent from paint, adhesive, degreaser, fresh concrete additive |
| Mist or fog | Condensation of vapour on airborne nuclei, or atomisation by spraying | Liquid droplets; evaporates, coalesces, or rains out on surfaces | Paint spray, cutting-fluid mist, acid mist from pickling and plating |
| Gas | Already gaseous at ambient conditions; also generated by reaction or combustion | Diffuses rapidly, fills the whole space, follows air currents and stratification | Carbon monoxide from engines and blasting; chlorine, ammonia, hydrogen sulphide in sewers |
| Fibre | Mechanical, from materials with a fibrous habit | High aspect ratio; settles slowly, aligns with airflow, penetrates deep into the lung | Asbestos in insulation and roofing; glass wool, rock wool, ceramic fibre |

A [fibre](#gt-2-fibre) deserves separate treatment: it is a solid particle
with an increased aspect ratio, and its special properties follow from that shape. Aerodynamic drag
depends mainly on the diameter, not the length, so a fibre tens of micrometres long can have the
aerodynamic size of a one-micrometre particle and reach the alveoli, where its length prevents the
macrophage from engulfing it. That geometry, not chemistry alone, explains why asbestos causes disease
where an inert dust of the same mass would not.

#### Particle size and what it decides

For any aerosol the single most useful descriptor is the aerodynamic diameter — the diameter of a
unit-density sphere that settles at the same speed. It predicts both where the particle deposits and
how long it stays airborne, and the conventional cut-offs used in occupational hygiene (standard
reference) are worth memorising because every dust control argument rests on them.

Only the fraction below about 5 µm — the [respirable fraction](#gt-2-respirable-dust) — reaches the alveoli, where there is no
mucociliary escalator to carry it back up. Alveolar macrophages engulf such particles but cannot digest
silica, asbestos or coal dust; the macrophage dies, releases the particle and its enzymes, and the same
particle is engulfed again. That cycle is the mechanism behind every pneumoconiosis, and it is why the
control target is the fine fraction, not the visible cloud.

#### How each form behaves, and what follows for control

Settling velocity rises with the square of particle diameter (standard reference), so a 10 µm particle
settles about a hundred times faster than a 1 µm one. Coarse dust contaminates the worker's immediate
area and can be contained close to the source; fine dust and fume travel with room air and must be
captured before they disperse.

Vapours are generated at a rate set by vapour pressure and temperature, so warming a solvent — a hot
degreasing bath, a freshly mixed resin — multiplies the release without any change in the quantity used.
Most solvent vapours are two to four times denser than air and sink into low places, which is why a
manhole, pit or trench can hold a lethal concentration while the air a metre above is acceptable.

* The form, not the chemical name, decides the route into the body and the control that catches it.
* Below about 5 µm a particle is respirable and reaches the alveoli; above 10 µm it is filtered by
  the nose.
* Fibres reach the alveoli because their aerodynamic diameter is set by width, not length; heavy
  vapours pool in pits, near-air-density gases mix throughout the space.

### 2.2Routes of entry and health effects

Four routes carry a chemical into the body, and each has a different geometry: the lung
is an enormous wet surface with direct access to blood, the skin is a barrier that some chemicals cross
easily, the gut is a portal with the liver immediately behind it, and a needle bypasses all three.

**Definition**

A [route of entry](#gt-2-route-of-entry) is the path by which a hazardous
substance crosses the body's outer boundary — inhalation, skin absorption, ingestion or injection. The
route fixes how much of the dose reaches the blood, and how fast.

#### Inhalation

Inhalation is the dominant occupational route, for a simple reason of surface area: the alveoli
present tens of square metres of thin, well-perfused membrane in direct contact with outside air, with
no filter and no first-pass metabolism. Airborne chemicals are breathed in through the mouth or nose,
and the size of the particles or droplets decides where the chemical settles in the respiratory tract.
Where it settles determines which symptoms or diseases develop — the point the deck makes explicitly,
and the reason dust sampling is size-selective.

For gases and vapours the controlling property is water solubility. A highly soluble gas — ammonia,
sulphur dioxide, formaldehyde, hydrogen chloride — dissolves in the moisture of the nose and upper
airway and produces immediate burning, lacrimation and bronchospasm: violent, but a warning, and the
injury stays where the gas was absorbed. A gas of low solubility — phosgene, nitrogen dioxide, ozone —
passes the upper airway almost untouched, reaches the alveoli, and produces pulmonary oedema hours
later, often after the worker has left the site and feels well. That delay is why low-solubility gases
are more dangerous than their smell suggests, and why exposed workers are kept under observation rather
than sent home.

#### Skin absorption

Some chemicals pass through intact skin and are taken into the body's system. The stratum corneum is
a lipid barrier, so the substances that cross it are the ones that dissolve in fat: organic solvents,
organophosphate and other pesticides, phenols, anilines, and a number of metal compounds. Two effects
then combine. Locally, the solvent defats the skin, producing dryness, cracking and contact dermatitis,
which both signals exposure and destroys the barrier. Systemically, the absorbed chemical enters the
venous blood and reaches the general circulation without passing through the liver, so the first-pass
detoxification that protects against an ingested dose does not happen. A splash on the forearm can
therefore deliver a systemic dose out of all proportion to its apparent size.

#### Ingestion

Chemicals that are swallowed are absorbed in the digestive tract. At work this is rarely deliberate;
it is hand-to-mouth transfer. Chemicals rub off dirty hands and contaminate food, drinks or tobacco
products; airborne dust settles on cups, plates and utensils and is then swallowed with the food. A less obvious path is soluble material trapped in airway mucus, carried up by the cilia and swallowed, so an inhaled dose becomes an ingested one. Because the swallowed dose passes through the liver, ingestion is often less acutely toxic than the same mass inhaled — but for substances activated by liver metabolism, such as many solvents and some pesticides, the liver increases the hazard instead of reducing it.

#### Injection

Injection is the smallest-volume route and the most efficient: a needlestick, a cut from a contaminated edge, a burst hydraulic hose or an airless sprayer forcing material through the skin deposits the substance directly into tissue or a vein, bypassing skin, lung and gut.

#### Local and systemic effects

Once inside, the effect may be local or systemic, and the distinction is examinable.

**Definition**

A [systemic effect](#gt-2-systemic-effect) is injury at a site distant from
the point of contact, produced after the substance has been absorbed and distributed by the blood. A
local effect is injury at the site of contact itself — a corrosive burn, a solvent dermatitis, a
conjunctivitis.

The same agent can produce both: chromic acid mist corrodes the nasal septum where it lands and, once absorbed, damages the kidney. Some effects are neither — a sensitiser such as isocyanate or formaldehyde provokes an immune response, so a dose far below the irritant threshold can trigger a severe reaction on re-exposure.

#### Acute and chronic

Acute effects are immediate reactions, appearing within minutes or hours of a single exposure:
narcosis from solvent vapour, tearing from a lacrimator, burning from an acid, headache and nausea from
carbon monoxide. They are usually reversible if exposure stops and are therefore visible — which makes
them, perversely, the less dangerous category. Chronic effects develop over years of repeated low-level
exposure, or long after it has ceased: the fibrosis of silicosis, the neuropathy of n-hexane, the
leukaemia of benzene, the bladder tumours of benzidine. Chronic effects are the reason occupational
hygiene works to exposure limits rather than to the point at which a worker complains.

| Target organ | Representative agents | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lung | Silica, asbestos, coal dust, welding fume, isocyanates | Pneumoconiosis, fibrosis, asthma, cancer |
| Skin | Solvents, epoxy resins, chromates, cement | Dermatitis, sensitisation, ulceration, cancer |
| Liver | Carbon tetrachloride, chloroform, vinyl chloride | Necrosis, cirrhosis, angiosarcoma |
| Kidney | Lead, mercury, cadmium, chlorinated solvents | Tubular damage, proteinuria, renal failure |
| Bone marrow and blood | Benzene, lead, carbon monoxide, nitrites | Aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, anaemia, methaemoglobinaemia |
| Nervous system | n-Hexane, carbon disulphide, organophosphates, mercury, lead | Peripheral neuropathy, tremor, cholinesterase inhibition, encephalopathy |
| Reproductive system | Lead, mercury, some solvents and anaesthetic gases | Reduced fertility, teratogenic and developmental effects |

Carcinogenic and teratogenic effects cut across this list and are assessed separately. A carcinogen is
treated as having no demonstrable threshold, so substitution and enclosure replace any “safe”
concentration; a teratogen acts on the developing fetus, which turns the exposure of a pregnant worker
into a specific risk assessment rather than a general one. Underneath every effect is dose —
concentration, duration, work rate and hence volume of air breathed, modified by individual
susceptibility — which is why limits are set for the population and health surveillance is kept for the
individual.

**Exam pointer**“Outline the routes of entry of chemicals into the body”
(5 marks) wants four named routes, one line of mechanism each, and one example. A list alone earns little: inhalation needs particle size and solubility, skin needs the lipid barrier and the bypass of first-pass metabolism, ingestion needs hand-to-mouth transfer, injection needs sharps.

* Inhalation dominates because the alveolar surface is large, thin and directly perfused.
* Water solubility of a gas fixes the site of injury: soluble attacks the upper airway at once,
  insoluble reaches the alveoli and causes delayed oedema.
* Skin absorption bypasses the liver, so a small splash can give a large systemic dose.
* Local means at the point of contact; systemic means after absorption and distribution.
* Acute is immediate and visible; chronic is delayed and is the real target of exposure control.

### 2.3Assessing and controlling chemical exposure

Chemical control is not a choice between ventilation and gloves; it is a sequence. First
find out what is there and how much of it people breathe, then remove it, then enclose it, then
administer around it, and only then dress the worker for what is left.

#### Assessment comes first

An exposure assessment answers four questions: which substances are present, in what quantity, who is exposed and for how long, and by which route. The inventory comes from purchase records, process knowledge and the safety data sheets; the estimate comes from personal sampling in the breathing zone, static samples for characterising a source, and direct-reading instruments where concentrations change quickly. Without that baseline any control is a guess and none can be shown to work.

#### The safety data sheet

**Definition**

A [safety data sheet](#gt-2-sds) is a technical document prepared by the
supplier or manufacturer of a material, providing detailed and comprehensive information on the health
effects of exposure, the hazard evaluation for its handling, storage and use, the measures needed to
protect workers at risk, and the emergency procedures.

The sheet contains much more information than the label, and it is the supplier's obligation to
provide one with the first shipment of any hazardous chemical. It may be written, printed or supplied
electronically, but its content follows a fixed sixteen-section order (standard reference), which is
what makes it usable: a reader who has never seen a particular product still knows that section 4 is
first aid and section 8 is exposure control.

| Section | Heading | What it settles on site |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Identification | Product name, supplier, emergency telephone |
| 2 | Hazard identification | Classification, pictograms, hazard statements |
| 3 | Composition and ingredients | Which constituent is actually dangerous |
| 4 | First-aid measures | What to do in the first ten minutes |
| 5 | Fire-fighting measures | Extinguishing medium, and what not to use |
| 6 | Accidental release | Spill kit, containment, evacuation distance |
| 7 | Handling and storage | Segregation, temperature, incompatibles |
| 8 | Exposure controls and PPE | Occupational exposure limits, glove and respirator type |
| 9 | Physical and chemical properties | Vapour pressure, density, flash point, solubility |
| 10 | Stability and reactivity | Whether it will polymerise, decompose or react |
| 11 | Toxicological information | Acute and chronic effects, target organs |
| 12 | Ecological information | Fate in soil and water, aquatic toxicity |
| 13 | Disposal considerations | Whether it can be sewered or must be incinerated |
| 14 | Transport information | UN number, packing group, tunnel restriction |
| 15 | Regulatory information | Which legislation applies to the substance |
| 16 | Other information | Revision date, and what changed since the last version |

Why it matters is practical rather than legal. Section 8 fixes the respirator and glove specification; section 9 explains whether the vapour will sink into a trench; section 6 tells the storeman what to do before the fire brigade arrives. A site that has the sheets but has never read section 3 will order the wrong glove — the hazardous constituent is often a minor ingredient, not the product on the label.

#### Labelling and pictograms

The label is the compressed version of the sheet: product identifier, supplier, pictograms, signal word, hazard and precautionary statements. The nine pictograms are flame, oxidiser, exploding bomb, gas cylinder, corrosion, skull and crossbones, exclamation mark, health hazard and environment. Reading them in combination is the point: a flame plus a skull tells the first-aider that the vapour is both flammable and acutely toxic, so the casualty is moved into fresh air and the source isolated before anyone approaches.

#### Inventory, storage and compatibility

Storage controls are unspectacular: keep the smallest quantity the job needs, hold it in a locked, ventilated store with secondary containment — bunding or a spill tray sized to the largest container — and keep incompatibles apart. Acids must not sit beside cyanides or sulphides, because the acid liberates
hydrogen cyanide or hydrogen sulphide; oxidisers must not sit beside solvents; water-reactive materials
must not sit where a burst pipe can reach them. Food, drink and allergens are stored separately from chemicals, with accurate ingredient listings on anything decanted.

#### Substitution and engineering control

After elimination, substitution is the most effective single measure: replace a chlorinated solvent
with an aqueous cleaner, a solvent-based adhesive with a water-based one, a dusty powder with a
pelletised or wetted product. Where the substance must stay, the process is enclosed as far as possible and the release is
captured at the point of generation by [local exhaust ventilation](#gt-2-lev):
a hood close to the source, a duct, a fan and a filter or discharge point. Capture velocity falls sharply with distance, so a hood moved 300 mm further from the work loses most of its ability to catch the plume.

General or dilution ventilation exchanges room air and suits only low-toxicity material released
steadily and uniformly. It is the wrong tool for dust, for fume and for vapours denser than air, all of
which are removed by capture, not by dilution. Enclosure and segregation are the other engineering forms: a sealed transfer point, a glove box, wet methods that suppress dust at source.

#### Administrative control, monitoring and PPE

Administrative controls change how the work is done rather than what it is done with: rotate workers to share the exposure, schedule maintenance and solvent work in off hours so a release affects fewer people, keep equipment maintained against leaks, enforce housekeeping and personal hygiene, and train people to know what is in the products they use and to use the least that will do the job. Monitoring closes the loop — repeat personal sampling after a change, and use biological monitoring where skin absorption makes air sampling understate the dose.

Personal protective equipment is the last line: it protects only the wearer, only while worn correctly and undamaged, and it fails silently — a respirator with a poor face seal gives no indication that it is leaking. Health surveillance, meaning baseline and periodic examinations for workers exposed to specific agents, is the check that the controls are holding. Emergency response is pre-planned around the sheet: eyewash and shower within reach of any corrosive, a spill kit matched to the material, and first aid taken from section 4.

**Exam pointer**“Describe the measures to protect from hazardous chemicals” (8 marks) is answered in hierarchy order, not as a list of ten ideas: elimination and substitution, then engineering (enclosure, LEV), then administrative (rotation, scheduling, training, monitoring), then PPE, then surveillance and emergency arrangements, with one line of justification per level. Cite the safety data sheet as the source of the information.

* Assess before controlling: inventory, exposure measurement, then the hierarchy of control.
* The safety data sheet has sixteen fixed sections; section 8 gives the exposure limits and PPE,
  section 9 explains the physical behaviour.
* Substitution removes the hazard; LEV captures it at source; dilution ventilation only dilutes.
* Storage is where most incidents begin — minimum quantity, bunding, segregation of incompatibles.
* PPE is the last line and protects only the wearer, only while it fits.

### 2.4Biological hazards: classification

Biological hazards are ranked by four questions — does it cause human disease, is it a
hazard to the worker, can it spread to the community, and can it be treated — and the answers set the
containment, not the other way round.

**Definition**

Biological agents are micro-organisms, including genetically modified micro-organisms, cell cultures
and human endoparasites, which may be able to provoke any infection, allergy or toxicity. A
[biohazardous agent](#gt-2-biohazardous-agent) is a biological agent that may be
able to provoke any infection, allergy or toxicity in those exposed to it.

Biological health hazards therefore include bacteria, viruses, parasites and moulds or fungi. They
threaten human health when they are inhaled, eaten, or come into contact with skin, and exposure can
produce sudden illness as well as long-term conditions, sometimes life-threatening. Biological hazards
exist in every work environment, but some sectors carry a much higher risk: healthcare, veterinary
services, agriculture, sewage management and laboratories. The activities that generate exposure are
equally specific — food production; any work in contact with animals or products of animal origin;
health care including isolation and post-mortem units; clinical, veterinary and diagnostic laboratories
other than diagnostic microbiology laboratories; refuse disposal plants; and sewage purification
installations.

Three kinds of harm need different controls: a worker may be **infected** by the agent;
exposed to **toxins** it produces, which act without infection taking hold; or suffer an
**allergic reaction** to the agent or its enzymes, the mechanism behind much occupational asthma in
baking, detergent manufacture and laboratory work. Exposure is either intentional, where the employee
works directly with the agent, or unintentional, where it is encountered because of the work: a
healthcare worker exposed to a blood-borne virus, a laundry worker injured by a needle left in linen, a
farmer infected by an animal disease that also affects humans.

#### The four groups

Agents are classified into [risk groups](#gt-2-risk-group) by level of risk of infection; learn the definitions almost verbatim, because single clauses carry the difference.

#### Why classification drives laboratory practice

The system rests on four criteria: is the agent pathogenic to humans, is it a hazard to the employee, is it transmissible to the community, and does effective treatment or prophylaxis exist. Each maps onto a control. Pathogenicity and hazard set the training, the cabinet class and the health surveillance. Transmissibility changes the building: a group 2 agent may be handled on an open bench, but a group 3 agent needs the laboratory separated by an airlock, held at negative pressure so that air flows inward, with HEPA-filtered exhaust — because the risk is no longer only to the worker inside. The availability of treatment decides how far vaccination and post-exposure prophylaxis can be relied on.

This is why [containment](#gt-2-containment) is described in two layers.
Primary containment protects the worker and the immediate environment — the cabinet, the sealed
centrifuge cup, the glove box, good technique. Secondary containment protects the outside world — the
room, the airflow, the access control, the waste route. Group 1 and 2 practice is built on primary
containment plus behaviour; group 3 and 4 practice adds secondary containment and engineering, because
the agent can no longer be assumed to stay inside the room.

cabinet class and the access rules.

* Biological agents include bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites; harm may be infection, toxin or
  allergy.
* Four groups, from “unlikely to cause human disease” to “severe disease, high spread risk, no
  effective prophylaxis”.
* Classification turns on four criteria, of which transmissibility to the community is the one that
  changes the building.
* Group 3 needs secondary containment — airlock, negative pressure, HEPA exhaust — because the risk
  extends beyond the worker.

### 2.5Bacterial, viral, fungal and parasitic agents

The four groups differ in one way that governs every control decision: whether the agent
can multiply outside a host, and how it survives the trip between hosts.

#### Bacteria

Bacteria are microscopic single-celled organisms that thrive in diverse environments — soil, the ocean, the human gut — and are found on all workplace surfaces and in the substances workers handle. They need four conditions to replicate: warmth, usually 5 to 60 °C; moisture; food, including foodstuffs and biological metalworking fluids; and a pH near neutral, most reproducing best at pH 7. Given all four they divide by binary fission roughly every twenty minutes, so one cell becomes millions within a shift. Bacteria are therefore the group that grows in the workplace: a spill, a stagnant cooling tower, a contaminated cutting-fluid sump or a warm water system becomes a reservoir, and cleaning, temperature control and drying are engineering controls, not housekeeping preferences.

#### Viruses

A virus is a small infectious agent that replicates only inside the living cells of an organism, and
it is the smallest of the microbes. It consists of a core of genetic material, either DNA or RNA,
covered by a protective protein coat, and it infects all forms of life. Because a virus has no
metabolism of its own, it cannot multiply on a bench or in a tank; it latches onto host cells, takes over
their functions and makes them produce more virus, and the infected cell then infects new cells. That
single fact sets the control strategy: disinfection and blood-borne precautions remove or inactivate
what is there, because nothing new will be generated outside a host — but a single needlestick can
inoculate enough virus to infect, so the emphasis falls on preventing the injury rather than on cleaning
up after it.

#### Fungi

A fungus is a primitive organism with a cell structure more like a plant or animal than a bacterium;
mushrooms, mould and mildew are the familiar forms. Fungi live in air, soil and water, some in the human body; only about half of all types are harmful. They reproduce by spores, dispersed by air until they reach a suitable place to develop. Spores are
the occupational hazard, because they are small enough to be inhaled and resistant enough to survive
drying: a mould growing on a damp wall, in hay, in compost or in accumulated bird droppings releases
spores when it is disturbed, and demolition, harvesting, composting and clearing are the operations that
disturb it.

#### Parasites

A parasite is an organism that lives in or on an organism of another species — its host — and derives
nutrients at the host's expense. Human endoparasites fall within the definition of a biological agent.
Their significance at work lies in the life cycle: many require an intermediate host or a period in soil
or water before they can infect, so the control is environmental — footwear, sanitation, safe water,
snail control in irrigation canals, drainage of standing water — rather than a vaccine or a respirator.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Bacillus anthracis* | Bacterium (spore-forming) | Anthrax | Animal handlers; handlers of carcasses, skins, hides and hair; wool and carpet processors |
| *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | Bacterium | Tuberculosis | Silica workers; workers exposed to heat and organic dusts; medical and laboratory personnel |

| *Aspergillus* species | Fungus (mould) | Aspergillosis, including allergic bronchopulmonary disease | Farmers, grain and compost workers, builders and cleaners in damp buildings |

  doubling makes reservoirs, not just contamination.

* Occupation follows agent: animals give anthrax and leptospirosis, dust gives tuberculosis, blood
  gives hepatitis B and HIV, water gives malaria and schistosomiasis.

### 2.6Infectious disease transmission and occupational exposure

Transmission route is the hinge between the organism and the control: an agent that
travels as a droplet nucleus needs air handling, one that travels in blood needs a sharps policy, and one
that travels in a mosquito needs drainage and repellent.

Six routes account for almost all occupational infection. **Airborne** transmission carries droplet nuclei — the dried residue of droplets, small enough to stay suspended and to be carried well beyond the room — and is the route of tuberculosis, measles and varicella; it is controlled by ventilation, negative-pressure rooms and respiratory protection, not by distance alone. **Droplet** transmission involves larger droplets that travel a metre or two and then fall, which is how influenza and pertussis spread and why the advice concerns close contact rather than the room. **Contact** transmission is direct skin-to-skin or
indirect through a contaminated surface, instrument or hand, and is the route of most healthcare-associated
infection and of ringworm. **Blood-borne** transmission requires the agent to be inoculated or to reach
a mucous membrane, and is the route of hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV. **Vector-borne** transmission
uses an insect or snail, as with malaria and schistosomiasis, so the control is the vector's habitat.
**Faecal-oral** transmission, including the soil stage of hookworm larvae, is controlled by sanitation
and footwear.

The deck's table of selected infectious diseases and occupations is the examinable form of the same idea, and the pairings are not obvious: tuberculosis is listed against silica workers and people exposed to heat and organic dusts as well as medical personnel, since dust-damaged lungs and crowding both raise the risk. Anthrax belongs to handlers of carcasses, skins,
hides and hair, including wool carpet processors. Tetanus belongs to farmers and anyone in contact with
manure, because the spores are in soil. Psittacosis belongs to pet shop personnel, gardeners, veterinary
surgeons and researchers — anyone near psittacine birds and poultry. Rabies belongs to veterinarians,
letter carriers, laboratory research workers and agricultural workers. Hookworm belongs to miners,
agricultural labourers, planters of sugar, tobacco, tea, rice and cotton, and brick and tunnel workers,
because the larvae enter through bare skin in wet soil.

| Anthrax | Animal handlers and handlers of carcasses, skins, hides or hair of infected animals, including wool carpet processors |

Exposure is not evenly distributed. **Vulnerable groups** include pregnant workers, because several agents cross the placenta or cause severe illness in pregnancy; workers whose immunity is reduced by illness, treatment or age; workers with open wounds or dermatitis, who offer the organism a direct route; non-immune workers, including new staff and those who have declined vaccination; and cleaners, laundry staff, porters and waste handlers, whose exposure is unintentional and who are routinely left out of training designed for clinical staff. Surveillance and vaccination policy have to be written around these groups, not the average employee.

### 2.7Control of biological agents at the workplace

Biological control follows the same hierarchy as chemical control, but the engineering
step is decisive: an organism that multiplies cannot be diluted away, so it has to be kept where it was
generated.

The principle has three parts: a harmful agent should be replaced by one that is not, or is less, dangerous in the present state of knowledge; exposure must be prevented; and preventive measures differ according to classification. Construction of the workplace, registration of exposures, notification of the authorities, training and collective protection all belong to that package. Where risks cannot be eliminated or substituted they are reduced: fewer exposed workers, engineering controls that minimise release, collective then individual protection, hygiene measures against accidental transfer, the biohazard sign, accident plans, testing outside the primary confinement, and safe collection, storage, transport and disposal of waste.

#### Engineering control

Engineering controls either remove the risk at source or isolate people from it, or both. The first
level is **enclosure**: access-controlled rooms, sealed processes, and the
[biological safety cabinet](#gt-2-bsc), in which inward airflow and a HEPA
filter protect the worker while a controlled downflow protects the work. Cabinet class follows the
group: a Class I cabinet protects the operator only, a Class II protects operator and product and is the
standard for group 2 and 3 work, and a Class III is a gas-tight glove box for group 4. The second level
is local exhaust ventilation with HEPA filtration, and mechanical ventilation that exchanges and filters
air through ducting, so that a known number of air changes per hour is achieved and air moves from clean
to dirty zones. In a group 3 suite the room is held at negative pressure relative to the corridor, so
that any leak flows inward; exhaust is HEPA-filtered and not recirculated.

#### Administrative control

Administrative measures change how the work is done. Vaccination policy for the specific hazards
present is the most effective single measure where a vaccine exists, and it is paired with health
surveillance and health risk assessment. Workers who are susceptible — non-immune, pregnant,
immunocompromised, or with broken skin — may have to be excluded from particular tasks, which is a
decision for occupational health rather than for the supervisor. Hygiene rules carry much of the load: no eating, drinking or smoking in contaminated areas; adequate washing and toilet facilities, with eye washes and skin antiseptics; protective clothing removed on leaving the work area and kept apart; equipment stored in a defined place, checked, cleaned and replaced, then decontaminated or destroyed. A sharps policy — no recapping, puncture-resistant containers at the point of use, immediate disposal — is the specific control for blood-borne infection, and safe systems of work, training and written accident plans complete the set.

#### PPE, disinfection and waste

PPE is used where the higher levels leave a residual risk. It protects only the wearer, only while it
is intact and correctly worn, and never the person standing next to them — which is why it is issued
after the cabinet and the airflow, not instead of them. Disinfection and waste handling are the tail of the process: autoclaving at the point of generation, chemical disinfection where heat would damage the item, sharps into rigid containers, labelled bags and then incineration, and safe transport for anything leaving the site. A spill is treated as an aerosol-generating event:
cover it, apply disinfectant, work from the outside inward, and never dry-sweep or use a high-pressure
hose. An exposure — a needlestick, a splash to the eye, a bite — is reported immediately so that
post-exposure prophylaxis, where it exists, is started within the window in which it works.

**Exam pointer**An eight-mark control question wants the hierarchy named and
then applied to biology: elimination and substitution, engineering (enclosure, safety cabinet, LEV with
HEPA, ventilation, access control), administrative (vaccination, exclusion of susceptible workers,
hygiene rules, sharps policy, training), PPE, then disinfection, waste and spill response. Say why the
engineering step matters more here than in chemical control.

* Substitute a non-pathogenic agent where possible; otherwise prevent exposure, scaled to the group.
* The safety cabinet is the core engineering control; its class follows the classification.
* Group 3 and 4 work adds secondary containment: negative pressure, airlock, HEPA exhaust.
* Vaccination, exclusion of susceptibles, hygiene rules and a sharps policy carry the administrative
  load.
* Autoclave at source, label the waste, and treat every spill as aerosol-generating.

### 2.8Noise: exposure, effects and control

Noise is unwanted sound, and the decibel is a ratio rather than a quantity — which is why
a change that looks small on paper is a large change in the energy reaching the ear.

Sound is a fluctuation in air pressure, and noise and vibration are the same physical phenomenon
distinguished by whether the ear detects it. The [decibel](#gt-2-decibel) (dB)
expresses sound pressure level on a logarithmic scale, so equal steps are equal ratios: an increase of
3 dB doubles the acoustic power, and about 10 dB is heard as roughly twice as loud.
An **A-weighting**, dB(A), filters the spectrum to approximate the ear's sensitivity and is used for average exposure; a **C-weighting**, dB(C), is used for peak, impact and explosive noise. Frequency, heard as pitch, matters independently: high frequencies are the more damaging, so two machines at the same dB(A) are not equally hazardous if one is dominated by a high-pitched whine. Whether the noise is continuous or impulsive matters as much: a steady level over a shift gives the ear time to react, while an impact — a cartridge tool, a drop forge, a hammer on steel — lasts milliseconds, too fast for the protective reflex, so one event can deliver the energy of a much longer steady exposure.

#### Exposure metrics and limits

The risk predictor is the [daily noise exposure level](#gt-2-lex8h),
LEX,8h — the time-weighted average over a nominal eight-hour day, an equivalent continuous level (Leq) over the shift — because damage depends on the total energy delivered — the product of level and
duration. The conversion between the two is the
[exchange rate](#gt-2-exchange-rate): a 3 dB exchange rate means that every 3 dB
increase halves the permitted exposure time, and a 5 dB rate means every 5 dB halves it. With the 5 dB
rate, 90 dB(A) is permitted for 8 hours, 95 dB(A) for 4 hours and 100 dB(A) for 2 hours. The regulatory
numbers follow from this. OSHA's permissible exposure limit is 90 dB(A) for an eight-hour day with a
5 dB exchange rate; NIOSH recommends keeping exposure below 85 dB(A) for eight hours with a 3 dB
exchange rate, and in 1981 OSHA required a hearing conservation programme for every employer whose
workers were exposed at or above 85 dB(A) time-weighted average over a shift. The 85 dB figure is
therefore an action level rather than a wall: below it, hearing damage is unlikely in most people; at
and above it, the employer must measure, protect, train and test. Instant irreversible damage begins at
a peak of about 130 to 140 dB(C) — explosions, firearm reports, electric arcs — which is the threshold
of pain at 140 dB(A).

#### Effects

The auditory effects are the important ones. Prolonged exposure above about 85 dB(A) damages the hair
cells of the inner ear, and because those cells do not regenerate the loss is permanent. The first stage
is a [temporary threshold shift](#gt-2-tts), or auditory fatigue — hearing is
dulled after exposure and recovers in 16 to 48 hours — and a worker who notices this at the end of every
shift is accumulating a permanent threshold shift, which is irreversible. Tinnitus, the ringing or
humming heard after leaving work, is an early warning of the same process. The non-auditory effects are broader than most workplaces allow for: annoyance and stress; disturbed sleep; elevated blood pressure; interference with speech communication; masking of warning signals, which makes noise a direct cause of accidents; effects on pregnant workers; and reduced concentration.

#### Control

Noise control is applied in three places, in order of effectiveness. **At source**: choose
low-noise tools and machinery, modify or replace the equipment, maintain and lubricate it — a worn
bearing or a loose guard turns a quiet machine into a noisy one — and change the way material is
handled, since dropping steel is louder than lowering it. **In the path**: enclose or isolate the
source, place a sound-reducing barrier or absorbent lining between source and worker, damp or isolate
structure-borne vibration so that the building stops acting as a loudspeaker, and use distance, which is
cheap and reliable — every doubling of the distance from a point source reduces the level by about
6 dB(A). **At the receiver**: hearing protection, which is the last line and the least effective,
because it protects only the person wearing it and depends on a seal that is easily broken by hair, a
hard hat or incorrect insertion. Earplugs attenuate best at low and very high frequencies, earmuffs at intermediate frequencies, active-cancelling muffs at low frequencies. Behind all of it sits the hearing conservation programme: measurement, audiometry before employment and at intervals, training, and records that allow a shift to be detected early.

## Safety in the Construction Industry

The trades that kill most often: height, scaffolds, welding, trenches, concrete, electricity and radiation.

**What the syllabus asks**Scaffolding and working platforms; welding and cutting; excavation; concreting; control measures. Electrical hazards – protection against voltage fluctuation – effects of shock. Radiation hazards – types and effects – disposal of radioactive waste.

### 3.1The construction industry and its accident profile

Construction is the second largest industry in the country after agriculture, and it kills people in a short list of repeatable ways — falls, collapse, vehicles, electricity — so the accident profile is the frame on which every control in this module hangs.

Construction covers establishments primarily engaged in building or engineering projects: new works, additions, alterations, and maintenance and repair. Its output is the country's infrastructure, and demand for it grows with industrialisation, urbanisation and rising expectations of living standards. It employs a very large number of people, but disperses them across thousands of short-lived sites — out of doors, at height, in holes in the ground, and alongside plant and live services.

| Sector | What it builds | Dominant hazards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Building construction | Houses, schools, hospitals, offices, industrial sheds | Falls from height, scaffolding, formwork collapse, hoisting |
| Heavy and civil engineering | Utility systems, land sub-division, highways, bridges, pipelines | Excavation and trenching, plant movement, dewatering, confined spaces |
| Speciality trades | One component of a project — masonry, painting, electrical, plumbing, glazing, steel erection | Tools, electricity, hazardous substances, work at height on someone else's edge |

The employment pattern is why the hazard profile is unusual. A factory has one workforce, one gate and one safety department; a construction site assembles a new organisation for each project, from several employers, for a few months, and then dissolves it. Trades follow one another into the same space — excavation, formwork, reinforcement, concrete, finishes — so the hazard a worker meets changes every week, and the person exposed to it is frequently not the person who created it.

#### Why small jobs are the most dangerous

The deck's blunt statement is that small building jobs are the most risky, and the mechanism is organisational rather than physical. A large project carries a safety officer, an induction, method statements, inspected scaffolding and a permit system, because the client and main contractor have the turnover to pay for them. A two-day job — a roof repair, a shopfront alteration, a water tank on a terrace — carries none of that: no induction, no scaffold, a borrowed ladder, one or two men who are also the supervisor, and work inside an occupied building. The same fall from the same height kills whether the job cost ten lakh or ten crore.

Two further patterns are worth memorising as rules. The first week on each new site is the most dangerous, because a worker who has not learned where the openings, fragile roof sheets, live cables and traffic routes are cannot avoid them; familiarity with the previous site is not familiarity with this one. Accidents are more frequent at the end of the day, when fatigue, failing light, the pressure to finish the pour and the shift handover coincide. Both point to supervision at the boundaries — the first day and the last hour.

#### Breakdown of fatalities by accident type

| Accident type | Share | Control it implies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Falls from height | 56% | Edge protection, hole covers, scaffold inspection, harness and running line |
| Trapped by something collapsing or overturning | 21% | Excavation support, propping of formwork, stable plant and stacks |
| Struck by a moving vehicle | 10% | Segregated routes, banksmen, reversing controls |
| Contact with electricity or electrical discharge | 5% | Isolation, earthing, GFCI, clearance from overhead lines |
| Struck by flying or falling objects during machine lifting | 4% | Exclusion zones, inspected slings, no one under a load |
| Contact with moving machinery or material being machined | 3% | Guarding, interlocks, lock-out/tag-out |
| Exposure to a hot or harmful substance | 1% | Substitution, ventilation, PPE, burns first aid |

#### What a properly organised site looks like

* Everyone can get to their place of work safely, by a defined route rather than a scramble over spoil and rebar.
* Edges from which people could fall have double guard rails or other suitable edge protection.
* Holes are protected with clearly marked and fixed covers, so that a cover cannot be kicked aside and an opening cannot be forgotten.
* The site is tidy — housekeeping is the removal of trip, fire and falling-object hazards.
* Lighting is good, including in stairwells, basements and temporary access.
* The site is fenced off from the public, who have no helmet and no reason to expect a hole in the pavement.

Each item is a design decision taken before work starts, not a behaviour to be requested afterwards. The personal protective equipment the deck names — helmet, high-visibility jacket, safety boots — prevents injury and death only in combination with those physical measures; a helmet does not stop a fall, it stops the consequences of a falling object. Lightweight shoes such as trainers or runners are not suitable on site: no toe cap, no penetration-resistant sole, no ankle support.

**Exam pointer**The seven percentages are the most quotable numbers in this module, and examiners expect them in order: falls 56%, trapped 21%, vehicle 10%, electricity 5%, falling objects 4%, machinery 3%, hot or harmful substance 1%. Add one control per line.

* Second largest industry after agriculture; three sectors — buildings, heavy and civil engineering, speciality trades.
* Falls 56%, collapse and overturning 21%, moving vehicles 10%, electricity 5% of fatalities.
* Small jobs are the most dangerous because they carry no organisation: no induction, no scaffold, no supervision.
* The first week on a new site and the end of the working day are the peaks of accident frequency.

### 3.2Working at height and ladder safety

Falls are 56 per cent of construction deaths, and almost all of them come from four openings the industry keeps failing to close — floor holes, platform gaps, shafts and stair walls — plus the ladder used where a scaffold was needed.

Working at height is not only roof work. The deck names the risks as holes in floors, gaps in working platforms, shafts and stair walls not adequately covered, barricaded or fenced off. Each is a defect of the works rather than of the worker's behaviour, and each is cheap to fix before the slab is cast and expensive afterwards.

* **Floor holes.** A service penetration or lift opening is sized exactly to accept a human body. It needs a cover that is marked, fixed down and strong enough to carry a person and any plant crossing it. A loose sheet of ply is not a cover; it is a trap that looks like a floor.
* **Platform gaps.** A gap between the scaffold deck and the building face wide enough to step into is a fall waiting for inattention. Gaps are closed, not bridged with a plank.
* **Shafts.** Lift shafts, ducts and risers run the full height of the building; they need fixed barriers at every landing.
* **Stair walls.** An unfinished stair with no handrail and an open well is a fall on the route people use most often, carrying tools and materials.

The deck's rules follow directly: never use a ladder or scaffold that is not properly fixed; never use incomplete scaffolding; make sure there are handrails and toe boards at all edges; wear a helmet, because things fall on sites. A fifth belongs with them: before starting work at height, check the clearance from any overhead power lines — the height that makes a fall dangerous is the height that puts a scaffold tube or a ladder into a live conductor.

#### Ladder safety

A ladder is access equipment, not a working platform. The deck's judgement is that ladders kill a lot of people, so the first question is not how to climb it but whether it is the right equipment at all: is the ladder right for the job, or would scaffolding be better?

If a ladder is used, five conditions must hold before the first foot goes on it.

1. **Right for the job.** Correct length, duty rating and type, long enough to reach the landing without standing on the top rungs.
2. **In good shape.** No cracked stiles, missing or bent rungs, damaged feet or corroded fittings.
3. **Secured near the top**, tied or otherwise fixed so that it cannot slip sideways or away from the face.
4. **On a firm base and footing** — both feet bearing on solid, level ground, never on spoil, boards, a pallet or wet clay.
5. **Rising at least 1 m beyond the landing place**, or with a proper hand hold, so that a person steps off holding part of the structure rather than transferring weight at the top rung.

The angle is set by that same geometry: too steep and the centre of gravity falls backwards so the ladder tips; too flat and the foot slides out under load. The conventional setting — a standard reference rather than a figure from the deck — is one unit out at the foot for every four of height, roughly 75 degrees to the horizontal.

#### Climbing and working on a ladder

* Always have a firm grip on the ladder and keep a good balance; both feet on rungs, body between the stiles.
* Never allow more than one person on a ladder at a time.
* Use tool belts or hand lines to carry objects — never carry a load in the hands, because one hand is then not holding the ladder.
* Do not lean out in any direction; move the ladder instead, since leaning puts the centre of gravity outside the stiles, which is the mechanism of most ladder falls.
* Do not allow others to work under a ladder in use, and if you have a fear of heights, do not climb one.

#### When a ladder is the wrong tool

The test is whether scaffolding would be better, and some cases always answer yes: when the job needs two hands for more than a moment — drilling overhead, fixing a bracket, handling glass; when the work lasts long enough for fatigue to matter; when materials must be lifted to the working position; when a side load will be applied, as when pulling cable or levering a shutter; when the work is close to overhead lines; and when the surface reached is a fragile roof.

#### Roof work and fragile materials

Roofs have their own statistics: 50 per cent of fatal injuries involving roofs are falls through fragile materials and 30 per cent are falls from edges and openings. The roof surface is the primary hazard, not the height above it. The deck's precautions are to inspect a roof before walking on it, to protect the edge, and to use proper harnesses and running cables on top of it; a harness clipped to nothing, or with slack enough to reach the gutter, changes nothing except the paperwork.

**Exam pointer**Height questions ask for precautions, not definitions: eliminate the height, prevent the fall (guard rails, covers, platforms), arrest the fall (harness, running line), then control access. The five ladder conditions — right job, good shape, secured, firm base, 1 m beyond the landing — are the most examined detail.

* Falls are 56% of construction fatalities; the standing hazards are floor holes, platform gaps, shafts and stair walls.
* Hole covers must be marked, fixed and strong enough to carry a person; loose ply is not a cover.
* A ladder needs a firm base, must be secured near the top, and must rise at least 1 m beyond the landing.
* One person per ladder, three points of contact, tools in a belt or on a hand line, no leaning out.

### 3.3Scaffolding and working platforms

The scaffold is the piece of temporary works that decides whether the 56 per cent of deaths from falls happen, and it is examined more often than any other topic in this module.

**Definition**

A [scaffold](#gt-3-scaffold) is a temporary structure erected to support access or working platforms, formed from individual tubes and joints or from proprietary components. It is used where work cannot be done at ground level or from a finished floor, so that workers have a safe, stable platform at the height of the work.

Scaffolding provides a temporary safe platform for maintenance, construction, repair, access and inspection. The related term is wider than the scaffold itself: a [working platform](#gt-3-working-platform) is any surface from which work can be carried out, or which serves as a means of access to a place of work, and it includes a scaffold, a suspended scaffold, cradles, roofs, floors, mobile elevating work platforms, step ladders and stairways. The duty therefore follows the platform, not the tube: a roof used as a workplace needs the same edge protection as a scaffold deck.

#### Hazards

* **Falling from the scaffold.** Missing guard rails, slipping on oil, ice or offcuts, inappropriate footwear, wet or windy weather — usually a housekeeping failure, not a structural one.
* **Struck by falling objects.** Tools, paint cans, bricks and offcuts drop from the lift above onto workers below and onto passers-by; a toe board and a fan or net are the physical answers.
* **Electrocution.** Welding and cutting from a scaffold put a live electrode and a long steel tube in one place, and an erected scaffold often brings a worker or a tube into contact with an overhead line.
* **Collapse.** Scaffolds not built to specification, missing ties or bracing, overloaded lifts, and for suspended scaffolds weak ropes, insecure attachment, or uninspected tiebacks, rope clips, counterweights, hoists and locking systems.
* **Overloading.** Excessive weight cracks the planks and overhanging material tips the platform; a scaffold is designed for a load class.

#### Erection, inspection and precautions

1. Erect only to a design, by trained scaffolders, on firm level ground with base plates on sole boards — never by the trade who will use it, and never on spoil or backfill.
2. Tie to the permanent structure as the scaffold rises; never leave a lift untied overnight.
3. Complete face and ledger bracing before any platform is loaded.
4. Board every platform fully, with no gaps and no unsupported overhang.
5. Provide top rail, mid rail and toe board at every exposed edge, including returns and lift ends.
6. Keep the deck clear of offcuts, cables, oil and mud, and of materials not in immediate use.
7. Respect the load class; never stack bricks or tiles on a lift and never let material overhang.
8. Provide a tied ladder or stair tower with a self-closing gate; never climb the standards or bracing.
9. Keep the scaffold clear of overhead power lines, and re-check the clearance before adding a lift or bringing plant near it.
10. Inspect and tag before first use, after alteration and after bad weather; remove the tag and bar access if the scaffold is incomplete.
11. For suspended scaffolds, inspect ropes, tiebacks, rope clips, counterweights, hoists and locking system before every use.

* Scaffold = temporary structure supporting access or working platforms; a working platform is any surface used for work or access.
* Standards vertical, ledgers along the face, transoms across, boards on transoms; ties hold it in, bracing stops it racking.
* Four types: independent tied, putlog, mobile tower, suspended or cradle; five hazards: fall from the lift, struck by falling material, electrocution, collapse, overloading.

### 3.4Welding and cutting

Welding concentrates heat, ultraviolet light, metal fume, compressed gas and mains electricity into one small workplace, so a welder meets more hazard types per hour than any other trade on site.

Welding joins metal parts by the application of heat, with or without pressure and with or without a filler material. The deck describes it as a complex process of heat and liquid metal transfer and chemical reactions, in which the joint forms by liquid metal deposition and then transforms on cooling into the solid state. The processes met on site are arc welding, where an electric arc between electrode and work provides the heat; gas welding and oxy-fuel cutting, where a flame does the work; and resistance welding, which uses pressure and current together.

| Hazard | What it does | Principal control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fume and gases | Respiratory illness, cancer, and impaired speech and movement — the last being the neurological effect of manganese in the fume | Local exhaust at the arc, general ventilation, respiratory protection, head kept out of the plume |
| Heat and light | Burns to skin and eyes, "arc eye" from ultraviolet, retinal damage from infrared | Helmet with the correct filter, screens around the work, leather gloves, apron, spats |
| Mechanical | Cuts, and crushed toes and fingers from cylinders, plate and clamps | Gloves, safety boots, handling aids, correct lifting |
| Electric shock | The most immediate and serious risk for a welder: sudden discharge through the body, injury or death | Dry gloves, insulated holder, sound cables, no welding in wet conditions, isolated set |
| Fire and explosion | Flammable material in the area is the number one cause of welding fires; fuel gas and oxygen cylinders add explosion | Clean area, removal or covering of combustibles, fire watch, extinguisher, cylinder discipline |

Fume is not one substance. Mild steel electrodes produce iron oxide fume, which causes metal fume fever — a flu-like illness a few hours after exposure; stainless steel produces chromium and nickel fume, including hexavalent chromium, a carcinogen; manganese in the wire produces a neurological syndrome resembling parkinsonism, which is what "impaired speech and movement" means. Because fume rises with the heat, the welder's breathing zone sits in the plume whenever the head is bent to the work, which is why extraction at the arc is worth more than a mask on the face.

Sparks and slag travel further than they look. The deck's rule is that if welding is to be done within 35 feet of flammable materials, a piece of sheet metal or a fire-resistant blanket must be placed over the material and a fire watcher kept nearby to track the sparks. The area is cleaned before welding starts, because flammable material around the working area is the number one cause of fire, and everyone on the job must know where the alarms, exits and extinguishers are. A Class ABC extinguisher is kept nearby with a full gauge; failing that, fire hoses or sand buckets.

Cylinders carry their own risks. Acetylene and oxygen are stored separately, upright and secured; oxygen fittings and hoses are kept free of oil and grease, because oxygen at pressure turns a smear of oil into an explosive mixture; flashback arrestors belong at regulator and torch; a cylinder that has been heated or damaged is cordoned and reported, not approached. Hot work inside a tank, pit or vessel is a confined-space entry as well as a fire risk, and needs a permit, testing and an attendant.

#### Safety precautions

1. Provide adequate ventilation and local exhaust to keep fume and gases out of the breathing zone and the general area.
2. Report concerns to a supervisor so that exposure to welding fume can be assessed.
3. Wear fire- and electricity-resistant clothing, hand shield or helmet, welding gloves, apron and boots — for heat, fire, shock and burns together.
4. Keep trouser legs uncuffed and covering the boot tops, since cuffs collect sparks; flame-retardant treatment becomes less effective with repeated laundering.
5. Wear earmuffs or earplugs against process noise.
6. Screen the work so that others are not exposed to arc radiation, and never look at an arc without a filter.
7. Perform lock-out/tag-out before any repair; only qualified technicians service welding equipment.
8. Keep a suitable Class ABC extinguisher nearby and check that its gauge is full.
9. Within 35 feet of flammable material, cover it with sheet metal or a fire-resistant blanket and post a fire watcher.
10. Work to a hot work permit where required, and inspect the area again after finishing, because smouldering starts late.
11. Never weld in wet conditions or with damaged cable insulation, holders or return clamps.
12. Store and handle cylinders upright and secured, oxygen and fuel gas separated, caps in place, no oil or grease near oxygen fittings.

**Exam pointer**Welding questions expect a hazard-by-hazard answer — fume, physical, electric shock, fire and explosion, each with its control. The quotable details are the 35-foot rule with a fire watcher, the Class ABC extinguisher and uncuffed trousers; examiners use them to see whether you read the source rather than reciting "wear PPE".

* Welding joins metal by heat, with or without pressure and filler; arc, gas and resistance are the site processes.
* Fume causes respiratory disease, cancer and manganese-induced neurological damage; extraction at the arc is the primary control.
* Electric shock is the most immediate risk; fire from flammables is the most common.
* Within 35 feet of combustibles: cover them and post a fire watcher.
* Class ABC extinguisher with a full gauge, and know the alarms and exits before the first arc is struck.

### 3.5Excavation

An excavation is the one workplace on a site created by removing the material that was holding the ground up, and it fails without warning — which is why the deck states flatly that there is no safe ground that will not collapse.

**Definition**

Excavation is work involving the removal of soil or rock from a site to form an open face, hole or cavity, using tools, machinery or explosives. It includes earthwork, trenching, wall shafts and tunnelling, and its construction uses are foundations, reservoirs and roads.

#### Hazards

| Hazard | How it arises | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collapse of the sides | Loss of lateral support; surcharge from spoil or plant at the edge; vibration; water; a dried cracked face | Batter, bench, shore or box; keep spoil and plant back; dewater |
| Materials falling in | Tools, spoil and bricks rolled or dropped over the edge | Toe boards or stop logs; no stockpile at the lip |
| Falls of people or vehicles | Open edge, unlit trench at night, vehicle reversing to the edge | Guard rail or barrier; stop blocks; lighting; banksman |
| Nearby structures collapsing | Undermined foundations, loss of ground through the face | Assess and support adjacent structures before digging |
| Damage to underground services | Buried cables, gas mains, water and telecom ducts struck by the bucket | Locate and mark, permit to dig, trial holes, isolate |
| Water ingress | Groundwater, a broken main, rain into an open cut | Dewatering wells and pumps; bunds and drains |
| Toxic or flammable gas | Gas leak, rotting fill, sewer connection, oxygen displacement in a deep cut | Test before and during entry; ventilate; no flame |

A trench collapses because soil has almost no tensile strength. What holds a face up is the friction between particles plus the lateral support of the ground that has been removed; once the face is exposed, only suction in damp clay and arching of the soil above keep it standing, and both disappear when the ground dries, when it rains, when a lorry passes, or when spoil is heaped at the lip. A cubic metre of soil weighs more than a tonne, so rescue by hand is usually too slow: the support goes in before entry, not after a crack appears.

#### Inspection and control

1. Inspect daily, and again after rain, after a freeze, after a vehicle has come close to the edge, and before each shift.
2. Know where every underground pipe and cable is before the bucket hits it: drawings, a cable detector, and hand-dug trial holes.
3. Work to a permit to dig, and isolate or divert any service that cannot be protected in place.
4. Batter, bench, shore or box every face deeper than 1.25 m, before anyone enters.
5. Guard rail or barrier around every excavation deeper than 2 m, with toe boards so material cannot be kicked in.
6. Keep spoil, materials and plant back from the lip — vehicles working too close, or rubble piled on the sides, may cause the collapse themselves — and use stop blocks for any vehicle tipping in, with a banksman for reversing.
7. Provide a secured ladder in the trench, extending above ground, so no one climbs the face or the struts.
8. Dewater with pumps and form bunds and drains so rain runs away from the cut.
9. Test for gas and oxygen deficiency before entry to any deep excavation, ventilate, and keep an attendant at the surface.
10. Support adjacent structures before digging below their foundations; light and fence the excavation at night.

**Exam pointer**The 1.25 m and 2 m figures are the marks most candidates lose: quote both, then explain one protection method properly rather than listing four.

* Excavation = removal of soil or rock to form an open face, hole or cavity: earthwork, trenching, wall shafts, tunnelling.
* Sides collapse without warning; support is installed before entry and inspected daily.
* Deeper than 1.25 m: shore or batter. Deeper than 2 m: guard rail or barrier.
* Four protection methods: battering, benching, shoring, trench box.

### 3.6Concreting

Concrete is a benign-looking material that burns, blinds, deafens and crushes, and its hazards arrive in a fixed order — dust at batching, alkali at pouring, weight at every stage.

Concrete, usually Portland cement concrete, is a composite of fine and coarse aggregate bonded by a fluid cement paste that hardens over time: durable, long-lasting and cost-effective, which is why it builds buildings, bridges, roads and most other infrastructure. The process has five steps, and each carries its own hazard.

| Step | What happens | Hazard introduced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Batching | Cement, coarse aggregate, sand and water are measured out | Cement and silica dust; handling bags |
| Mixing | Materials are mixed until the paste is uniform in colour and consistency | Dust, noise, rotating plant, alkaline splashes |
| Transporting | Fresh concrete is moved to the site and placed in the formwork | Vehicle movement, pressurised pump lines, spillage |
| Compaction | Air is removed by poker vibrator to increase strength | Hand-arm vibration, noise, electric shock from the vibrator |
| Curing | Moisture is retained so hydration can complete | Wet, slippery surfaces; standing water at open edges |

#### Hazards

* **Cement burns.** Wet cement is strongly alkaline and caustic; a splash left on the skin can produce third-degree burns, and the classic case is concrete that has run into a boot or soaked a trouser leg and stayed there for the shift. The burn develops over hours and is often painless at the start, because the alkali damages nerve endings.
* **Dermatitis.** Prolonged contact with wet cement, and the hexavalent chromium in it, produces irritant and allergic contact dermatitis — dry, cracked, bleeding skin on hands and forearms.
* **Dust.** Aggregate dust and cement powder are dispersed when bags are emptied and when concrete is cut or ground; the respirable fraction reaches the alveoli, and the silica in the aggregate is the same hazard that causes silicosis elsewhere.
* **Slips.** Wet concrete is slippery until it dries, and a film on boots or a scaffold board is enough to bring a person down at an edge.
* **Manual handling.** Cement bags, rebar bundles and formwork panels are heavy and awkward; back injury from overexertion is a common site injury.
* **Formwork and falsework collapse.** Fresh concrete is a fluid weighing about as much as dense stone, and the formwork and props carry that load while it is still liquid; a missing prop, an unbraced leg or a premature strike puts the whole pour down on the people below.
* **Pumping, vibration and noise.** A blocked pump line that is opened, or a hose that whips when the pump starts, discharges concrete at pressure; poker vibrators expose the hands to hand-arm vibration; plant raises the noise for everyone on the pour.
* **Falling slabs.** Concrete slabs can weigh more than 800 pounds and are at risk of tipping, shifting and falling on those around them during lifting and stacking.

#### Control measures

1. Wear waterproof gloves, a long-sleeved shirt, full-length trousers and proper eye protection, and never let slurry stay inside boots or against the skin.
2. Wear an approved hard hat on site, and shatterproof eye protection at all times to keep cement, particles, dust and fumes out of the eyes; no contact lenses on the job.
3. Wash exposed skin promptly, use barrier cream where provided, and report any persistent rash — dermatitis is treatable early and chronic later.
4. Wear self-fitting earplugs of waxed cotton, foam or glass fibre wool against noise; there is no cure for noise-induced hearing loss.
5. Wear steel-toed safety boots against falling objects, crushing and punctures from rebar and tie wire.
6. Lift correctly: back straight, knees bent, load close to the body, and do not lift too much — use two people or a mechanical aid.
7. Control dust at source with wet methods, enclosed silos and local exhaust, and provide face masks or respirators where cement dust cannot be suppressed.
8. Design, erect and inspect formwork and falsework as a structure; do not strike props before the concrete has the strength the designer specified.
9. Keep pump lines sound, never open a pressurised line, and keep everyone clear of the delivery end when the pump starts.
10. Provide warning signs and guards wherever cement hazards are present, and clear spillage before it is walked through the site.
11. Control vibration by rotating tasks and using low-vibration tools, and keep the vibrator's cable and plug sound.

**Exam pointer**Concreting is usually a short part of a larger question. The marks are in the pair of mechanisms: wet cement is caustic and causes third-degree burns, and cement dust is a respirable silica hazard. Name the five process steps, then give a numbered control for each — PPE alone does not earn the dust marks.

* Five steps: batching, mixing, transporting, compaction, curing — each with a hazard.
* Wet cement is alkaline and caustic: third-degree burns, especially inside boots and clothing.
* Aggregate and cement dust are respirable; silica is the same hazard as in other trades.
* Falsework carries the full weight of liquid concrete; a missing prop collapses the pour.
* Controls: waterproof gloves and full cover, eye and hearing protection, steel-toed boots, correct lifting, dust suppression.

### 3.7Electrical hazards

Electricity is the one energy source on a site that gives no warning before it kills: it is invisible, it is silent, and the current that stops a heart is smaller than the current that lights a lamp.

Electricity is the movement of free electrons between atoms; current is the movement of electrical charge; resistance is opposition to current flow; voltage is a measurement of electrical force. Conductors have little resistance, insulators such as dry wood, rubber, glass and Bakelite have high resistance, and grounding is a conductive connection to the earth that acts as a protective measure.

#### The six hazards

The deck groups electrical injuries as BESAFE — burns, electrocution, shock, [arc flash](#gt-3-arc-flash) or arc blast, fire, explosions — and adds a seventh consequence that is often what actually kills.

| Hazard | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Burns | The most common shock-related injury; three types — electrical (direct contact), arc flash (current through the air), thermal contact (ignited combustible material) |
| Electrocution | Fatal exposure to a lethal amount of electrical energy; "to kill with electrical shock" |
| Shock | The body becomes part of the circuit, and the reflex response to current through it |
| Arc flash and arc blast | Energy released through air across a high-voltage gap: thermal radiation and intense light at up to 35,000°F, plus a pressure wave from rapidly heated air |
| Fire | Most electrical fires come from fixed wiring; cords, plugs, receptacles and switches also cause them |
| Explosion | Electricity ignites an explosive mixture of material in the air |
| Fall | A shock on a platform, ladder or scaffold throws the worker off it; the fall, not the current, often kills |

The deck states the scale in two facts about young workers: electrocution is the cause of 12 per cent of all workplace deaths among young workers, and the third leading cause of work-related deaths among 16- and 17-year-olds. It also cites 156 electrocutions in 2014, up from 141 in 2013.

#### How a shock happens

A shock requires a circuit, and the body supplies the missing link. When a person touches a live part while standing on earth, or touches two parts at different potentials, the body completes the path from the source through hand and trunk to the feet and the ground. Contact potential is the voltage between the part touched and the earth at the point of contact. [Step potential](#gt-3-step-potential) is the voltage between the two feet: during a fault, current spreads outward through the soil and the surface carries a voltage gradient, so a person standing with feet apart bridges two points at different voltages and is shocked without touching anything live. This is why the rescue rule for a fallen conductor is to shuffle or hop away rather than stride.

Four factors decide how bad the shock is: the pathway through the body, the amount of current, the length of exposure, and whether the skin is wet or dry. Water is a great conductor, allowing current to flow more easily in wet conditions and through wet skin. A dry, calloused palm may have enough resistance to limit the current from a low-voltage source; the same hand, wet, will not.

#### Effects of current on the body

| Current | Reaction |
| --- | --- |
| Below 1 milliampere | Generally not perceptible |
| 1 milliampere | Faint tingle |
| 5 milliamperes | Slight shock felt; not painful but disturbing. Average individual can let go. Strong involuntary reactions can lead to other injuries |
| 6–25 milliamperes (women) 9–30 milliamperes (men) | Painful shock, loss of muscular control. The freezing current or "let-go" range: the individual cannot let go, but can be thrown away from the circuit if extensor muscles are stimulated |
| 50–150 milliamperes | Extreme pain, respiratory arrest, severe muscular contractions. Death is possible |
| 1,000–4,300 milliamperes | Rhythmic pumping action of the heart ceases. Muscular contraction and nerve damage occur; death likely |
| 10,000 milliamperes | Cardiac arrest, severe burns; death probable |

Read the table as three thresholds. At 1 mA the current is at the edge of sensation. At 5 mA it is unpleasant but the average person can still let go, so 5 mA is where involuntary reaction — not tissue damage — starts causing falls and knocks. At 10 mA the current is inside the [let-go](#gt-3-let-go) band: the flexor muscles of the forearm contract harder than the extensors can oppose, the hand closes on the conductor, and release is impossible, so the exposure continues until something else breaks the circuit. At 50 to 100 mA the current arrests breathing, and above that the heart's rhythm is at risk. The mechanism throughout is that current overrides the body's own electrical signalling: the diaphragm tetanises, cardiac muscle loses its coordinated rhythm and fibrillates, and tissue along the path is cooked.

The deck's warning in capitals is the one to carry out of the section: low voltage does not mean low hazard. A 230 V supply drives tens of milliamperes through a wet hand with ease — past the let-go threshold and into the range where breathing stops — and most site and domestic fatalities are at mains voltage, not transmission voltage.

**Exam pointer**"Explain the common electrical hazards on construction sites" (5 and 8 marks) wants the six types with a mechanism each, the four factors that decide severity, and the current-effect table. Quote three numbers — 5 mA can let go, 50–150 mA arrests breathing, 1,000 mA and above stops the heart.

* Six hazards, BESAFE: burns, electrocution, shock, arc flash or blast, fire, explosion — plus falls caused by shock.
* A shock needs a circuit: body from hand to feet, completed through earth.
* Severity depends on path, current, duration and wet or dry skin.
* 1 mA tingle, 5 mA releasable, 10 mA let-go, 50–150 mA respiratory arrest, 1,000 mA and above death likely.

### 3.8Protection against electrical hazards and voltage fluctuation

Protection rests on four principles — keep the current away from the body, give it a harmless path if it escapes, disconnect it fast when it does, and know the distance at which it can jump.

#### Protection methods

1. **Insulation.** It prevents conductors from contacting each other or a person. Never repair a damaged cord with tape, never hang a cord on a nail, and never run one through a door or window where it will be crushed.
2. **Double insulation.** Two layers of protection and no earth connection needed; marked "Double Insulated" with the square-within-a-square symbol. Not indestructible, and not a substitute for a GFCI.
3. **[Earthing](#gt-3-earthing).** A conductive connection to earth eliminates unwanted voltage and gives leakage current a low-resistance path back to the source, so the protective device operates and the casing does not sit at line potential. Removing the ground pin to fit an old socket removes that safety feature.
4. **[GFCI](#gt-3-gfci) or RCD.** Protects people rather than equipment: it detects a ground fault — current going somewhere other than back through the return conductor — and interrupts the circuit, limiting the duration of the shock. Three types: receptacle, temporary or portable, and circuit-breaker GFCI.
5. **Fuses and MCBs.** These protect the cable, not the person. A current of 50 mA through a person will never blow a 16 A fuse; that is the gap the RCD fills.
6. **Isolation and lock-out/tag-out.** LOTO prevents unexpected energisation while equipment is worked on, prevents contact with operating parts such as blades, gears and shafts, and prevents the unexpected release of hazardous gases, fluids or solid matter.
7. **Clearance from overhead lines.** Survey the site, never store materials under lines, and keep tools and equipment at least 10 feet away. The covering on a line is for weather protection only: touch a power line, covered or bare, and death is probable.
8. **Competent persons and identification.** Mark every breaker for the circuit it protects, every disconnect for the equipment it serves, and label all voltages.
9. **Tools and PPE.** Inspect cords before use, use correctly rated extension cords, never carry a tool by its cord, remove damaged tools from use. Hard hats, rubber insulating gloves and insulating clothing, with the glove rating matched to the work; metal hard hats must not be worn near lines.
10. **An assured equipment grounding conductor programme** and scheduled inspection, so that the earth path is proved rather than assumed.

| --- | --- |
| Up to 50 | 10 |
| Over 50 to 200 | 15 |
| Over 200 to 350 | 20 |
| Over 350 to 500 | 25 |
| Over 500 to 750 | 35 |
| Over 750 to 1,000 | 45 |
| Over 1,000 | As established by the line owner or a registered professional engineer qualified in transmission and distribution |

#### Voltage fluctuation

No supply is perfectly steady. Voltage fluctuation is any departure from the nominal value, in four forms: a dip or sag, where voltage falls for a few cycles to seconds; a swell, where it rises; a transient or surge, a very short spike; and sustained over- or under-voltage. Harmonic distortion and phase imbalance belong to the same family, because both change the voltage the equipment actually sees.

The effects divide between equipment and protection. An induction motor fed at reduced voltage draws more current to produce the same torque, so it runs hotter and its insulation ages faster; contactors and relays chatter or drop out, stopping a pump or hoist without warning; electronic controls in drives, weighing systems and chargers reset or fail; and a surge stresses insulation until it punctures, which is how a transient becomes a short circuit.

On protection devices the effect is less obvious and more dangerous. A fuse and the thermal element of an MCB respond to current over time, not to voltage, so a sustained over-voltage heats the cable while the device sees a normal current; a transient can weld the contacts of a contactor or degrade a breaker so that it no longer trips; and an RCD or RCBO with electronic sensing can be left by a dip holding closed but no longer responding. The result is protection present on the board and absent in fact.

The controls follow. Size cables for voltage drop as well as current, protect sensitive equipment with a voltage stabiliser, and protect the installation with surge protection at the distribution board rather than at the socket. Use motor protection that senses current and temperature, not voltage alone, and keep the earthing sound. The deck's rule against consumer power strips and plug-in surge protectors on site belongs here: they daisy-chain connections, hide an overloaded outlet, and their surge element is no substitute for protection at the board; the deck's alternative is a three-way extension with a GFCI.

**Exam pointer**For a protection question, give the measures in the order a designer would: insulate, earth, protect with an RCD, protect the cable with a fuse or MCB, isolate with LOTO, keep the clearance distance, and only then PPE. Memorise the clearance table — 10 feet up to 50 kV rising to 45 feet at 750–1,000 kV — because a question asking for "safe distance" expects the number.

* Insulation, earthing, RCD, fuse or MCB, LOTO, clearance, competent person, tools and PPE — in that order of reliability.
* Earthing gives leakage current a path home; an RCD protects people, a fuse protects cable.
* 10 feet minimum from lines up to 50 kV, rising to 45 feet at 750–1,000 kV.
* Voltage fluctuation damages equipment through heat and insulation stress, and can leave protection devices present but not working.

### 3.9Radiation hazards

Radiation is the one hazard in this module that cannot be seen, felt or smelled, so the whole control strategy rests on knowing which type it is, how far it travels, and what dose the body has absorbed.

**Definition**

[Ionizing radiation](#gt-3-ionizing) is any electromagnetic or particulate radiation capable of producing ion pairs by interaction with matter — energy released by atoms as electromagnetic waves (gamma or x-rays) or as particles (alpha, beta, neutrons). The spontaneous disintegration of atoms is radioactivity, and the excess energy emitted is a form of ionizing radiation.

The dividing line that matters is energy: ionizing radiation has enough to remove electrons from atoms, non-ionizing radiation does not.

Non-ionizing radiation — ultraviolet, visible light, infrared, radio waves, microwaves — lacks that energy and only excites atoms; it heats tissue and, after prolonged exposure, can inflame the eye, burn skin, cause tumours or melanomas and reduce fertility. Its site sources are welding arcs, lasers, heat lamps and transmitters. Ionizing sources are radiography of welds, density gauges, medical and research sources and the nuclear fuel cycle.

Particulate radiations are particles with mass — alpha and beta particles, electrons, neutrons; electromagnetic radiations, x-rays and gamma rays, have no mass and no charge. That single difference explains the shielding behaviour: a particle with mass and charge interacts constantly with the material it crosses and loses energy quickly, while a photon with neither passes through until it happens to collide.

**Alpha particles** are clusters of two neutrons and two protons, identical to a helium nucleus and about 7,500 times the mass of an electron. Being large and heavy they interact frequently with the electrons of atoms and lose energy rapidly: they cannot penetrate a piece of paper or the dead layer of skin. That makes them harmless outside the body and extremely dangerous inside it, because an inhaled or swallowed alpha emitter deposits all its energy in a few cells. Radium and plutonium are alpha emitters.

**Beta particles** are electrons travelling at very high energy. They travel faster than alpha particles and, depending on their energy, can do much damage; on the skin they cause severe burns known as beta burns. Beta emitters are present in the fission products of reactors, and some, such as iodine-131, are given internally to patients to diagnose and treat disease.

**X-rays** are photons emitted from electron orbits, as when an excited orbital electron falls back to a lower energy orbit. **Gamma rays** are photons emitted from the nucleus, often in radioactive decay; they have no mass or charge, travel in straight lines, and typically carry more than 100 kilo electron volts per photon — more than 200,000 times the energy of visible light.

#### Units

| Quantity | Unit | Relationship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity | Becquerel (Bq) = one disintegration per second | 1 curie (Ci) = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second |
| [Absorbed dose](#gt-3-absorbed-dose) | rad = 100 ergs absorbed per gram of any material; 1 gray (Gy) = 1 joule per kilogram | 1 Gy = 100 rad |
| Equivalent dose | rem = dose in rads × quality factor (QF) | 1 [sievert](#gt-3-sievert) (Sv) = 100 rem |

#### Effects on the body

Biological effects begin with ionization: the radiation removes electrons from the atoms of tissue, and the resulting ions and free radicals break bonds, including those in DNA. A cell may repair itself and operate normally, die and be replaced by normal biological processes, or repair itself incorrectly and reproduce at an uncontrolled rate — the origin of cancer.

Dose rate decides which outcome dominates. A **chronic dose** is a relatively small amount received over a long period; the body tolerates it better because only a small percentage of cells need repair at any one time and dead cells can be replaced. This is the type of dose received as occupational exposure. An **acute dose** is a large dose in a short time — 1 rad or greater to the whole body within a few days at most — producing effects observable within hours to weeks.

| Effect | Threshold in the deck |
| --- | --- |
| Radiation sickness; blood-forming organ (bone marrow) syndrome — internal bleeding, fatigue, infection, fever | Above 100 rad |
| 50% of an exposed population die within 60 days without medical attention | Above 450 rad, whole body |
| Gastrointestinal syndrome — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration, loss of digestion, bleeding ulcers | Above 1,000 rad |
| Central nervous system syndrome — loss of coordination, confusion, coma, convulsions, shock | Above 5,000 rad |

The ordering is anatomical: the fastest-dividing cells fail first, and nerve cells, which do not reproduce, fail only at the highest doses. **Somatic effects** appear in the exposed person — prompt effects soon after an acute dose, such as temporary hair loss about three weeks after 400 rad to the scalp, and delayed effects years later (cancer, cataracts). **Genetic effects** appear in future generations through damage to reproductive cells, which is why the limits protecting the worker also protect their children; the embryo and fetus are the most radiosensitive stage, particularly in the first 20 weeks.

#### Protection

Three variables control every exposure. **Time**: dose is proportional to duration, so it falls if the job is short and rehearsed. **Distance**: dose falls sharply with distance from a point source, so a step back or a longer handling tool does more than a thicker apron. **Shielding**: the right material — paper or a glove for alpha, a few millimetres of aluminium or plastic for beta, dense lead or thick concrete for gamma and x-rays — between source and body. Monitoring closes the loop, because an exposure that is not measured cannot be controlled.

**Exam pointer**Give the two families (particulate: alpha, beta; electromagnetic: x-ray, gamma), the penetration of each with its shielding, then the units and the acute/chronic distinction. The mark-earning numbers are 100 rad for radiation sickness, 450 rad for 50% mortality in 60 days, 1 Gy = 100 rad and 1 Sv = 100 rem.

* Ionizing radiation removes electrons from atoms; non-ionizing only excites them.
* Alpha: paper or dead skin; beta: a few millimetres of aluminium; gamma and x-ray: thick lead or concrete.
* 1 Gy = 100 rad; 1 Sv = 100 rem; 1 Bq = one disintegration per second; 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq.
* Chronic dose is repairable, acute is not: sickness above 100 rad, 50% mortality above 450 rad in 60 days.

### 3.10Radioactive waste

Radioactive waste is classified by how much heat and activity it carries, and that classification, not the material's origin, decides how and where it is disposed of.

Nuclear waste is composed of radionuclides and is segregated into low, medium (intermediate) and high level. High level waste is produced in nuclear reactors and consists of fission products, which have short half-lives, and actinides, which have long ones.

| Class | Description | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low level (LLW) | Very low concentration of radioactivity; no shielding needed during normal handling and transport; typically material used to handle the highly radioactive parts of reactors | About 90% of the volume |
| Intermediate (ILW) | Requires shielding but needs little or no provision for heat dissipation; chemical sludge, resins, metal fuel cladding and other reactor products | About 7% of the volume |
| High level (HLW) | Large amount of radioactivity and thermally hot; fissionable elements from reactor cores and transuranic waste; mostly Pu-238, 239, 240, 241, 242, Np-237 and U-236; spent fuel if declared a waste | About 3% of the volume but 95% of the radioactivity; growing by about 12,000 metric tons a year |

**[Transuranic waste](#gt-3-transuranic)** is any waste containing transuranic alpha-emitting radionuclides with half-lives longer than 20 years. The definition turns on two properties that decide the route: alpha emission, harmless outside the body but dangerous if dispersed as dust and inhaled, and a long half-life, which means the isolation must last for geological rather than engineering time.

#### Where it comes from

Hospitals and nuclear medical centres produce mainly low level solid, liquid and gaseous waste. Solid: used molybdenum-technetium generators, empty vessels, swabs, syringes, gloves, laboratory clothing, bench covers. Liquid: washing from active laboratories and the excreta of injected patients. Gaseous: work with tritium and tritiated water, iodine and xenon-133.

#### Management

The Indian philosophy rests on four phrases: **delay and delay**, holding waste until short-lived activity decays; **dilute and disperse** for material below the cleared level; **concentrate and contain** for what must be isolated; and **recycle and reuse** where it can be done safely. No waste is released to the environment unless it is cleared, exempted or excluded from regulation, and the emphasis is on minimisation and volume reduction.

Management runs through a fixed sequence — segregation, characterisation, handling, treatment, conditioning, monitoring — before final disposal. High level waste takes three stages in India: it is [vitrified](#gt-3-vitrification), immobilised in inert borosilicate glass; stored in an engineered interim store for passive cooling and surveillance until it qualifies for disposal; and finally disposed of in a deep geological repository. The multi-barrier principle runs through all of it — waste form, container, backfill and rock — each preventing the movement of radionuclides back to the human environment.

#### Disposal

| Option | What it is | Applies to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Near-surface | Engineered channels or vaults on the ground surface or up to a few metres below | Low level waste |
| Caves, vaults, silos | At least a few tens of metres below ground, and up to a few hundred metres | Intermediate level waste |
| Geological repository | Tunnels, vaults or silos in a chosen formation at least a few hundred metres down; first used in 1999 in the US | High level waste |
| Deep boreholes | The geological concept taken kilometres deep rather than hundreds of metres, with further isolation from groundwater; can be sited near power plants; one is being created in Finland | High level waste |
| Launch into space | Removes waste from the biosphere — "infinite storage space" — but with a high risk of launch failure, a limited volume per launch and a high energy cost; about $20,000 per kg to orbit | Not an operating route |
| Sea disposal | Packaged waste dumped in the deep ocean, formerly practised and now prohibited under the international conventions on dumping at sea | Historic only |

Disposal is the final step, and its aim is safety through emplacement in facilities designed for the appropriate level of containment and isolation, using natural and engineered barriers over long periods. The objections follow the same logic. Near-surface facilities depend on institutional control lasting as long as the waste remains hazardous, which for long-lived nuclides is longer than any institution. Geological repositories depend on the geology being predictable for a hundred thousand years, and on a political process that can choose a site. Space depends on a launch record that is not perfect, and sea disposal on the ocean not returning what is put into it, which it does: the packages corrode and marine organisms take up the radionuclides.

Monitoring runs through every stage — characterisation before treatment, the facility and its groundwater after emplacement, and the interim store until the waste moves.

* LLW is 90% of the volume; 3% is HLW, and HLW carries 95% of the radioactivity.
* Transuranic waste: alpha-emitting, half-life longer than 20 years.
* Philosophy: delay and delay, dilute and disperse, concentrate and contain, recycle and reuse.
* HLW: vitrification in borosilicate glass, engineered interim storage, deep geological disposal.
* Options: near-surface (LLW), vaults and silos (ILW), geological repository and deep borehole (HLW); space and sea disposal rejected on risk and on law.

### 4.1Air pollution: definition, sources and classification

Air pollution is the hazard students think they already know; the marks lie in the definition, the
taxonomy, and in why no process can ever be made emission-free.

**Definition**

Air pollution is the presence of one or more contaminants, or combinations of them, in such quantities and
for such durations as may be or tend to be injurious to human, animal or plant life, or to property, or which
unreasonably interferes with the comfortable enjoyment of life or property or the conduct of business.

Each clause carries a separate idea. *Quantity and duration* make the pollutant a dose rather than a substance: the same gas is part of the atmosphere at background concentration and a hazard at ten times that. *Injurious to property* covers corrosion and soiling, which often costs more than the health bill. *Interference with enjoyment* covers nuisance — odour, haze, lost visibility — which injures nothing but reaches the control board first.

#### Why every process pollutes

Pollution follows from the second law, not from carelessness. No process converts all of its input into saleable output, and what is left leaves as waste — as gas or fine dust, through the air. A boiler 85 per cent efficient has put 15 per cent of its fuel energy elsewhere, some of it as carbon monoxide and soot; coal carries mineral matter that becomes ash; a reaction 95 per cent selective makes 5 per cent of the wrong molecule. There is no zero-emission process, only one whose emissions have been moved, diluted or converted — hence four levers: change the fuel, change the process, capture the pollutant, disperse what remains.

#### Natural sources

The deck's list is worth reproducing as a set: wind-blown dust from ground with little or no green cover; gases
released by body processes, including carbon dioxide from respiration, methane from cattle during digestion and
oxygen from plants during photosynthesis; smoke from burning inflammable material; and volcanic ash and acid
gases. Lightning and forest fires add oxides of nitrogen. Natural sources set the baseline against which
man-made pollution is judged — which is how the deck can attribute about two-thirds of atmospheric sulphur
oxides to volcanic and other natural sources and still treat sulphur dioxide as an industrial pollutant. They are
spread across the planet, whereas man-made sources concentrate where people live, and concentration, not mass,
causes disease.

#### Man-made sources

Smoke dominates again: from biomass, factories, vehicles and furnaces, that is, every form of combustion that
is not tightly controlled. Landfill waste generates methane as it decomposes anaerobically. Besides energy
production, the sectors responsible are petrochemicals, chemicals, minerals and metal processing, with smaller
contributions from water treatment, food processing, dry cleaning and garages. Their emissions split in two:
combustion of fuel oil, gas oil and natural gas, giving sulphur and nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and
particulates; and non-combustion processes such as solvent evaporation, acid mist and crushing dust.

#### Primary and secondary pollutants

**Definition**

[Primary pollutants](#gt-4-primary-pollutant) are emitted directly from an
identifiable source in an already harmful form — sulphur dioxide, particulate matter, oxides of nitrogen,
volatile organic compounds, carbon monoxide, lead. [Secondary
pollutants](#gt-4-secondary-pollutant) are never emitted; they form in the atmosphere when primary pollutants react with one another or
with other constituents of the air.

The distinction decides where control is possible. A primary pollutant can be captured at the end of the pipe;
a secondary one cannot, because by the time it exists the pipe is kilometres behind, leaving only its precursors
to attack. Sulphuric acid mist and photochemical smog are the deck's two examples. Sulphur dioxide oxidises in the
gas phase or in cloud droplets and the acid that results is what lands on the statue and the crop. Nitrogen
oxides and volatile organic compounds cooked in sunlight give ground-level ozone and fine secondary aerosol —
ozone is a pollutant at ground level and a shield in the stratosphere (§4.5).

#### Point and non-point emissions

**Definition**

Point-source emissions leave through a discrete conveyance — a chimney, vent or duct — at a measurable
location. Non-point, or [fugitive](#gt-4-fugitive-emission), emissions escape from a
large area: field sources such as tanks and pools, and leaks from valves, taps and couplings.

Fugitive emissions are the harder problem and the more dishonest line in a plant's accounting, because they
never appear in a stack test. A breathing tank farm, a weeping flange, a conveyor transfer point throwing dust —
each is invisible to the chimney monitor, and together they can exceed the stack.

Placing the pollutant on this source–pathway–receptor chain is what makes control strategy possible: a measure
is only ever an intervention at one of the three numbered links.

#### Indoor air

Outdoors a mass of vapour is diluted by wind and by a mixing layer hundreds of metres deep; indoors the same
mass is trapped in a few tens of cubic metres at perhaps one air change per hour, so the steady concentration is
far higher. Cleaning products and paints release their solvents into that volume, and cooking or heating with a
poor flue adds combustion products to it. A house can have worse air than the street outside it.

**Exam pointer**"Categorize various causes of air pollution" is a 5-mark question and
rewards grouping over listing: fossil-fuel combustion, industrial processes, agriculture, mining and construction,

only man-made ones.

* Pollution is a mass-balance problem: whatever a process does not convert to product leaves as waste.
* Primary pollutants can be captured at the stack; secondary ones can only be prevented by cutting precursors.
* Point sources are measurable and controllable; fugitive sources are neither, and are often larger.
* Indoors the same mass of vapour gives a far higher concentration because no wind dilutes it.

### 4.2Major pollutants and their health effects

Six pollutant families carry almost all the marks: carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, the oxides of
nitrogen and sulphur, hydrocarbons and non-methane volatile organic compounds, and particulate matter.

#### Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and tasteless, which is why it kills. The deck gives no effect at the
normal atmospheric concentration of about 0.1 ppm and serious effects above it; transport contributes about 75 per
cent of the man-made total, wood burning 10 per cent, industry 15 per cent.

The mechanism is competitive binding. Carbon monoxide forms carboxyhaemoglobin in preference to oxygen, so
fewer sites remain to carry oxygen, and the curve shifts so that the oxygen carried is released less readily to tissue. The damage is not in the lung but in every tissue downstream; heart, brain and retina fail
first because their demand is highest.

#### Carbon dioxide

Sources are fossil-fuel combustion, burning of any kind, and jet aircraft. At ordinary concentrations it is not
toxic; in a confined space a high concentration causes headache and nausea and asphyxiates by displacing oxygen.
Its weight here is climatic — the greenhouse link that returns in §4.6, where every clean-coal technology is
judged on how much of it reaches the stack.

#### Oxides of nitrogen

The family contains nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide and nitrous oxide. Sources are fuel combustion in
automobiles and industries, lightning, forest fires and natural ionising radiation. Combustion dominates, and it
works by heat rather than fuel composition: at flame temperature the nitrogen and oxygen of the air combine
directly. That is why low-NOx burners stage the flame instead of cleaning the coal.

Nitrogen dioxide is only slightly soluble, so it passes the nose and reaches the small airways and alveoli,
inflaming and sensitising them — the deck's lung problems, concentrated in those with existing respiratory
disease. Its second listed effect, reduced blood-carrying capacity, comes from nitrite oxidising haemoglobin to
methaemoglobin, which carries no oxygen.

#### Oxides of sulphur

Sulphur dioxide and trioxide come mostly from sulphur in fuel. The deck attributes about 67 per cent of
atmospheric sulphur oxides to volcanic and other natural sources, the rest to fossil-fuel burning, transport and
industry — but the industrial share is emitted where people are.

Sulphur dioxide is highly soluble, the mirror image of nitrogen dioxide: it is absorbed in the nose, throat and
large airways within the first few centimetres of the tract, dissolving to bisulphite and sulphite and provoking
bronchoconstriction. Asthmatics respond at concentrations that leave healthy adults unaffected.

#### Hydrocarbons and non-methane volatile organic compounds

The hydrocarbon family covers methane, ethylene, acetylene and the terpenes, from coal fields, natural fires,
incomplete combustion, forest fires and agricultural burning. Effects are carcinogenicity in some members; the
formation, with nitrogen oxides and sunlight, of ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate, both far more phytotoxic than the
hydrocarbons that made them; and damage to plants, rubber and paints.

Non-methane volatile organic compounds exclude methane and come from vegetation, biomass burning, geogenic
sources and human activity. Being lipid-soluble solvents they cross membranes easily and are metabolised in the
liver to reactive intermediates, so the effects read like a solvent list: carcinogenicity in some compounds;
liver, kidney and central nervous system damage; eye, nose and throat irritation; headache, loss of coordination,
fatigue and dizziness.

#### Particulate matter

Particulate material is any solid particle or droplet small enough to stay suspended — aerosols, dust, smoke,
fumes, mist, fog. The deck's sources are diesel trucks and buses, construction equipment, power plants and
wildfires: mostly combustion, mostly at ground level.

Size governs everything. Particles above about ten micrometres are caught in the nose and cleared by the
mucociliary escalator. The fine fraction below about two and a half micrometres reaches the alveoli, where there
is no escalator and clearance is by macrophage only — the deck's point that smaller particulates do greater
damage because they penetrate deeper. The effects follow the depth: premature death,
aggravated asthma, acute respiratory symptoms, chronic bronchitis, decreased lung function, with existing heart
disease, the elderly and children most at risk.

#### Lead and other heavy metals

Lead causes poisoning and at high concentration damages liver and kidney; ammonia, the deck's agricultural
by-product, belongs on the same industrial list. The mechanism is specific: it blocks
the enzymes that build haem, so the first sign is anaemia, and because it competes with calcium it accumulates in
bone and hits the developing nervous system in children.

#### What decides how badly a person is affected

The deck lists five factors, the first three belonging to the exposure and the last two to the person: nature of
the pollutant, concentration, duration of exposure, state of health of the receptor, age group of the receptor.
Solubility decides where in the airway the dose lands — a soluble gas such as sulphur dioxide gives up its mass in
the nose and throat, a less soluble one travels deeper; particle size decides the same for
dust.

* Solubility decides depth of deposition in the airway; particle size decides it for dust.
* Carbon monoxide harms by displacing oxygen from haemoglobin, not by irritating anything.
* Nitrogen oxides are made by heat, not by fuel; sulphur oxides by sulphur in the fuel.
* Hydrocarbons do much of their damage after conversion to ozone and PAN, not as emitted.
* The same concentration is a nuisance to an adult and an admission for an asthmatic child.

### 4.3Effects on animals, plants and materials

The same pollutant that inflames a lung bleaches a leaf, acidifies a soil and eats a limestone
facade, and each of those three is a separate exam question with its own mechanism.

#### Plants

Plants are injured through the stomata, and that single fact explains most of the pattern. A gas enters the leaf
by diffusion through the stomatal pores, dissolves in the thin film of water wetting the internal cell walls, and
there becomes an acid or an oxidant in direct contact with living tissue. Because the stomata are open in
daylight, injury is worst in bright, warm, humid weather — which is also when the pollutant is most likely to be
photochemically generated. Roots are spared; leaves take the dose.

Three named symptoms carry the marks. [Chlorosis](#gt-4-chlorosis) is the loss or bleaching of chlorophyll, so the leaf turns pale yellow-green; photosynthesis is limited by the pigment that captures light, so the plant is starving before it looks sick. [Necrosis](#gt-4-necrosis) is the death of patches of tissue, seen as brown dead spots, and it is irreversible because the cells are dead rather than depleted. Abscission is premature falling of leaves, the plant discarding tissue it can no longer maintain.

The whole-plant effects follow from the leaf effects and are what the farmer notices: suppressed growth because the carbohydrate budget is smaller, premature ageing, stunted vegetables, reduced yield, and worse damage to young plants, which have less stored reserve. The distinction that matters in a crop-damage claim is between visible injury and hidden loss — a plant can lose a fifth of its yield with barely a mark on it.

#### Acid deposition and soil acidification

[Acid deposition](#gt-4-acid-deposition) is the transfer of acidic compounds from the
atmosphere to the ground, as wet deposition in rain, snow, fog and mist, or as dry deposition of the gases and
particles themselves. Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides are oxidised to sulphuric and nitric acid, in the plume,
in cloud droplets or on the surfaces where they finally settle. The result is rain with a pH well below the
natural value — the deck's benchmark for fresh water is a natural pH of about 6 to 8, and acid deposition pushes
precipitation below that range.

In soil the acid works indirectly and slowly, which is why the damage is regional rather than local. Hydrogen ions displace the base cations — calcium, magnesium, potassium — held on clay and humus, and those nutrients leach away with the mobile sulphate and nitrate anions. Aluminium, harmless when locked in mineral structures, becomes soluble at low pH and is toxic to fine roots, so the tree loses its nutrient supply and part of its root system at once. In lakes the same aluminium clogs fish gills.

#### Animals and birds

Animal injury mirrors the human pattern at lower intensity. The primary action is on the mucous lining of the
respiratory tract, which is irritated and thickened, producing bronchitis and asthma in livestock kept near a
source; the deck's homely indicator is lack of appetite in pet animals, which is what a vet sees before any
diagnosis. Acid deposition damages aquatic life, so a heron or an otter loses its food base before it loses
anything else. Severe air pollution also hampers the migration of seasonal birds, which is a behaviour effect
rather than a toxic one but is real, and it matters because a disrupted migration is a population effect that
takes years to show.

#### Materials

The material damage is the most visible economic loss. Acid deposition corrodes metals and
eats stone on statues and monuments, decolours buildings and cloth, and penetrates concrete to reach the
reinforcement; the corrosion products occupy more volume than the steel they replaced, so the concrete spalls and
the member loses section without ever being overloaded.

The chemistry is the same in each case. Sulphur dioxide with oxygen and moisture becomes sulphuric acid, which reacts with limestone and marble — calcium carbonate — to form calcium sulphate, soluble and recrystallising with a larger volume, so the surface crumbles rather than staining. Ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate crack rubber and insulation by attacking the double bonds in the elastomer. Sulphur dioxide, ozone, hydrogen sulphide and aerosols damage protective coatings, so the protection fails before the substrate does. Particulate soiling is not only aesthetic: a dark facade absorbs more heat and drives further moisture damage.

| Ozone and PAN | Necrotic flecks; premature ageing and abscission | Cracks rubber and electrical insulation; damages paints |

* Gases enter through stomata and dissolve in the leaf's internal water film; roots are not the entry route.
* Chlorosis is reversible loss of pigment; necrosis is irreversible death of tissue.
* Visible injury and yield loss are different measurements, and yield loss occurs without symptoms.

### 4.4Air pollution control

Air pollution cannot be prevented entirely, only controlled — and the choice of control is decided
by whether the pollutant is a gas or a particle, and by how small the particle is.

#### Control at source

Prevention is always cheaper than capture, because a pollutant that is never formed needs no device, no reagent,
no power and no disposal route. The deck's three preventive measures are the whole of this idea. Selection of a
suitable fuel removes the precursor at the start: low-sulphur coal in a power plant, or compressed natural gas
instead of diesel, reduces sulphur dioxide and particulate at once. Modification of the industrial process attacks
the formation mechanism instead of the emission — low-NOx burners, described in §4.6, are the standard
example. Selection of a suitable site and zoning for the industrial unit does not reduce a gram of emission, but it
puts the source where the plume will disperse before it reaches people, which is the only reason zoning works at
all.

#### Control measures

Where source control is impossible the deck lists four options: collect the pollutant with
equipment, destroy it by thermal or catalytic combustion, change it into a less toxic form, or release it through a
tall chimney. The last is the weakest — it converts a local problem into a regional one, since the mass is
unchanged and deposition merely happens further away.

#### Particulate removal

The **settling chamber** is a widened duct in which the gas velocity drops low enough for gravity to settle
the dust. It costs almost nothing to run and has no moving parts, and it removes only the coarse fraction — the
grit that would choke anything downstream. It is used as a pre-cleaner, not as a final device.

The [cyclone separator](#gt-4-cyclone-separator) makes the gas spin, and the particles,
having more inertia than the gas, are thrown to the wall and fall out at the bottom. It is compact, dry, cheap to
run and tolerant of high temperature, and it captures the medium fraction; its weakness is that the fine fraction
follows the gas out, because a small particle has too little inertia to be thrown across the short distance
available.

The **fabric filter**, or baghouse, passes the gas through woven or felted fabric that intercepts particles
by sieving, impaction and diffusion; a dust cake builds on the fabric and does most of the collecting, and the bags
are cleaned by reverse pulse. It achieves very high efficiency even on fine dust, and its limitations are
temperature — the fabric degrades — moisture and condensation, and the fact that a torn bag fails silently.

The [electrostatic precipitator](#gt-4-electrostatic-precipitator) charges the particles
in a strong electric field and collects them on oppositely charged plates, from which they are dislodged by
rapping. It handles very large gas volumes at low pressure drop and the deck gives its efficiency as 99 per cent. It
is the standard choice for a coal-fired boiler, and its limits are cost, floor area, and a dependence on the dust's
electrical resistivity — a dust that holds charge too well or too poorly is collected badly.

#### Gas removal

The wet scrubber brings the gas into contact with water or an alkaline slurry in a spray tower, venturi or packed bed. Particles are captured by impaction on droplets and gases by absorption, so one device does both jobs; the penalty is a liquid effluent, a saturated plume and the fan power for the pressure drop. With limestone slurry it becomes a flue-gas desulphurisation unit, and §4.6 quotes the deck's 99 per cent for that.

For carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons, which are not particles and not water-soluble, the device is a
**catalytic converter** or a thermal afterburner. The converter passes the exhaust over a precious-metal
catalyst that oxidises carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons to carbon dioxide and water and reduces nitrogen oxides to
nitrogen. Its limitation is that the catalyst is poisoned by lead and needs a narrow band of air-fuel ratio to
work at all, which is why it depends on unleaded fuel and closed-loop control.

* Prevent at source first: fuel choice, process change, siting — in that order of cost.
* Gravity and centrifugal devices are pre-cleaners; filters and precipitators do the fine work.
* Electrostatic precipitation and limestone flue-gas desulphurisation are both quoted at 99 per cent.
* A tall stack disperses; it does not remove, and it regionalises the problem.
* Every capture device creates a solid or liquid waste that must itself be managed.

### 4.5Depletion of the ozone layer

Ozone depletion is the one global atmospheric problem with a working solution, and the exam wants
the catalytic mechanism, the Antarctic hole, and the difference between a thinning and a hole.

**Definition**

[Ozone depletion](#gt-4-ozone-depletion) is the gradual thinning of the ozone layer in
the upper atmosphere, caused by the release of chemical compounds containing gaseous chlorine or bromine from
industries and other human activities. The ozone layer itself is the region of the stratosphere holding a high
concentration of ozone, which absorbs 97 to 99 per cent of the harmful ultraviolet radiation reaching the earth
from the sun.

The distinction that carries marks is thickness against season. The layer has thinned globally — the deck cites about 5 per cent over 1979 to 1990 — and it also thins sharply, annually and temporarily, over the poles, where large losses occur each spring and recovery follows in summer. That event is the *ozone hole*: not a gap, but a region where the ozone column has fallen by more than half. It forms over Antarctica because the polar vortex is cold enough in winter to make ice crystals on which chlorine is converted into a form that destroys ozone as soon as spring sunlight returns.

#### The catalytic cycle

Chlorofluorocarbons are stable, unreactive and insoluble, which is exactly why they were used as refrigerants,
propellants and solvents — and exactly why they are dangerous. Nothing in the lower atmosphere destroys them, so
they survive the decade or more needed to drift up into the stratosphere. There, ultraviolet light of short
wavelength has enough energy to break the carbon–chlorine bond, and a chlorine atom is released.

That atom then runs a two-step cycle. It attacks ozone and takes an oxygen atom, giving chlorine monoxide and
ordinary oxygen; the chlorine monoxide then meets a free oxygen atom and gives the oxygen back, regenerating the
chlorine atom and making another molecule of oxygen. The chlorine is not consumed. It is a catalyst, and the same
atom repeats the cycle thousands of times before it is eventually removed, which is why a small mass of CFC does
enormous damage — the ozone is destroyed, the chlorine is unchanged, and only the ozone column records the loss.

#### Culprits

The [ozone-depleting substances](#gt-4-ozone-depleting-substance) are the compounds that
release chlorine or bromine when exposed to strong ultraviolet light. Those containing chlorine are the
chlorofluorocarbons, carbon tetrachloride and the hydrochlorofluorocarbons; those containing bromine are the
halons, methyl bromide and the hydrobromofluorocarbons. CFCs are the most abundant, and they were released from
solvents, spray aerosols, refrigerators and air conditioners — which is why the household refrigerator and the
fire extinguisher are the examples every answer should contain.

Nitrogenous compounds — nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide and nitrous oxide — also deplete ozone, and nitrous oxide
matters because it is long-lived and increasing with fertiliser use. Natural processes contribute as well:
sunspots, stratospheric winds and volcanic eruptions. The deck's own figure is that natural causes account for no
more than 1 to 2 per cent of the depletion, which is the number to quote when an answer needs to apportion blame.
The deck also notes unregulated rocket launches as a growing cause, on the argument that they may deplete more than
CFCs do; treat that as the deck's claim rather than as settled science.

#### Effects

Less ozone means more ultraviolet-B at the surface. In humans it produces skin disease, skin cancer, sunburn, rapid ageing and a weakened immune system — the immune effect being the one that matters most where infectious disease is already a burden. In animals it causes skin and eye cancer and hampers the development of larvae in transparent, shallow-living species such as amphibians. In plants it reduces growth, flowering and photosynthesis, forests included. In the sea it strikes the base of the food chain: plankton are damaged, and every organism above them follows.

#### Solutions

The answers are substitution and recovery. CFCs give way to hydrofluorocarbons, which contain no chlorine and so do not attack ozone; halon extinguishers are replaced; CFC aerosols and solvents are eliminated; chlorine- and bromine-releasing cleaning products give way to benign ones; nitrous oxide emissions are controlled; refrigerant is recovered during servicing instead of vented. The legal instrument is the [Montreal Protocol](#gt-4-montreal-protocol) of 1987, which phased out CFCs, halons and then HCFCs, and is why the stratospheric chlorine burden is now falling. One caveat: the common substitutes are potent greenhouse gases, so the ozone problem was solved by moving part of the burden to the climate problem.

**Exam pointer**"Investigate the causes, effects and solutions for ozone layer depletion" is
7 marks and divides cleanly: one mark for the definition and the 97 to 99 per cent absorption, three for the
catalytic cycle with chlorine regenerated, two for effects grouped as human, plant and marine, one for the Montreal
Protocol and substitution.

* Ozone absorbs 97 to 99 per cent of incoming ultraviolet; a 5 per cent thinning was measured between 1979 and 1990.
* The hole is seasonal and polar, not a permanent gap.
* Chlorine is a catalyst: it is regenerated, so trace concentrations do large damage.
* CFCs are the most abundant ozone-depleting substance; natural causes account for only 1 to 2 per cent.
* Substitutes solved the ozone problem while adding to the greenhouse problem.

### 4.6Clean coal combustion technology

Clean coal is a family of technologies that make coal burn more efficiently and more acceptably, not
a fuel that has been made clean — and the exam rewards the distinction.

**Definition**

[Clean coal technologies](#gt-4-clean-coal-technology) are technologies designed to
enhance the efficiency of conversion and the environmental acceptability of coal extraction, preparation and
use. The definition is deliberately about the whole chain, because washing the coal, burning it better and
cleaning the flue gas are three different engineering problems.

Coal survives because it is the cheapest fuel per unit of heat delivered to a power station. That single economic
fact explains why a technology suite this expensive is pursued at all, and it is also why the honest verdict at the
end of this section matters: the alternatives are not being compared on cost alone.

#### Before combustion: washing

Coal washing, or beneficiation, grinds the coal into pieces and separates it from its mineral matter by gravity.
The deck's description is a barrel of liquid at a chosen density: the coal floats and the impurities sink. The
principle is that clean coal is less dense than shale and pyritic sulphur, so a suspension of the right specific
gravity separates them without any chemical reaction at all. Washing removes ash and part of the sulphur before it
ever reaches the furnace, which reduces both the particulate load and the sulphur dioxide that the flue-gas plant
must later capture. It also reduces the mass that has to be transported, which is why washeries sit at the mine
rather than at the station.

#### During combustion

**Low-NOx burners** attack the formation mechanism rather than the emission. The deck describes
three stages. In the first, combustion occurs in an oxygen-deficient zone, where nitrogen oxides would normally
form; starving the flame of oxygen suppresses that formation. In the second, hydrocarbons are formed which react
with the nitrogen oxides already present, reducing them to nitrogen. In the third, internal air staging completes
the combustion — but this stage can itself generate fresh nitrogen oxides, which is why the deck's final condition
is that combustion be completed in an air-lean environment. The whole device is a compromise between finishing the
burn and not making new NOx while doing it.

**Fluidised bed combustion** burns crushed coal in a bed of inert particles kept suspended by an upward air
stream, so the bed behaves like a boiling liquid. The turbulence gives intimate contact between coal and air at a
lower and more uniform temperature than a pulverised-fuel flame, which suppresses nitrogen oxide formation, and the
bed can accept limestone mixed in with the fuel so that sulphur is captured in the bed itself as calcium sulphate.
The pressurised version, PFBC, puts the whole bed under pressure so that the hot gas can drive a gas turbine as
well as a steam turbine, raising the efficiency of conversion.

#### After combustion

Flue-gas desulphurisation is the limestone route the deck describes: limestone and water are sprayed into the
flue, sulphur dioxide reacts with calcium carbonate to form gypsum, calcium sulphate, which is collected and sold
for construction. The deck gives the effectiveness as 99 per cent, and the sale of the by-product is what keeps the
economics tolerable. Particulates are removed by electrostatic precipitation, also quoted at 99 per cent, as
described in §4.4.

#### Gasification and IGCC

Gasification does not burn the coal. It reacts coal with oxygen and steam to make a synthesis gas, mostly hydrogen with some carbon monoxide, which is cleaned and burned to turn a turbine; as the hot gas cools it heats water for a second turbine. That two-turbine arrangement is the [integrated gasification combined cycle](#gt-4-igcc), attractive because cleaning a small volume of fuel gas before combustion is cheaper than cleaning a large volume of flue gas after it. The deck is candid: emissions improve, efficiency reaches up to 45 per cent, but the technology is unproven at scale, only demonstration units exist, the gas is cleaned cold when hot cleaning would be better, and it is costly although existing plants can be modified.

#### Carbon capture and storage

Capture addresses the one emission that no flue treatment removes: carbon dioxide. The gas is separated,
compressed and stored underground, a route the deck calls geosequestration. Its problems are stated plainly. Carbon
dioxide is hard to capture; once captured it must be liquefied, and liquefaction takes about 20 per cent of the
energy the coal yielded; the storage site must be monitored for as long as the gas is there; there are only so many
geological formations suitable for mass storage; and transport to them is costly. A capture plant therefore pays an
energy penalty at the front end and a monitoring liability at the back.

#### Is clean coal clean

The deck's answer is unambiguous, and it is the answer to give: there is no way to make coal clean. Gasification

### 4.7Water pollution: sources, pollutants and the oxygen measures

Water pollution is judged by what it does to the oxygen budget of the receiving water, which is why
three laboratory numbers — BOD, COD and dissolved oxygen — carry most of the technical marks.

**Definition**

Water pollution is the contamination of water bodies — lakes, rivers, oceans and groundwater — usually by human
activity: any change in the physical, chemical or biological properties of water that has a detrimental consequence
for living things. It occurs when pollutants are discharged directly or indirectly into a water body without
sufficient treatment to remove the harmful compounds.

The deck calls it the second most important environmental concern alongside air pollution, and the sources divide
as they do for air. Point sources discharge directly at a known place: oil wells near water bodies, factories,
power plants, underground coal mines. Non-point sources have no single location — runoff from agricultural fields,
lawns, gardens, construction sites, roads and streets, carrying whatever the surface happened to hold. As with air,
the non-point source is the harder one, because there is nothing to put a pipe on and nothing to sample.

#### The pollutant families

| Family | Examples | What it does to the water |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inorganic salts | Nitrates, phosphates, carbonates, chlorides, sulphates of calcium, magnesium, iron | Hardness, scale in pipes, iron stains on fabric; nitrates and phosphates feed algal blooms |
| Acids and alkalis | Effluent from soap making, textile dyeing, leather tanning | Pushes pH outside the natural 6 to 8; directly toxic to fish |
| Organic matter | Sewage, food-processing waste, slaughterhouse washings | Feeds micro-organisms, which consume dissolved oxygen |
| Suspended solids | Soil, grit, mineral fines | Turbidity; settles and smothers the bed; bank deposits decompose and smell |
| Floating matter | Oil, grease, foam-forming compounds | Unsightly; blocks light; hinders the water body's self-purification |
| Thermal discharge | Cooling water from power plants and boilers | Warm water is lighter, so the body stratifies; bacterial action speeds up and oxygen falls |
| Toxic chemicals | Cyanides, sulphides, solvents, hexavalent chromium | Cumulative poisoning of aquatic life; chromium soaks through skin and sensitises it |
| Pathogens | Bacteria and viruses from domestic sewage, food plants, slaughterhouses | Water-borne disease in everyone downstream |
| Radioactive material | Isotopes from nuclear reactors and medicine | Ionising radiation; not detectable by ordinary contaminant tests; mutation and cancer |

#### Dissolved oxygen, BOD and COD

Organic pollution is not toxic in itself. It is food, and the bacteria that eat it use dissolved oxygen from the
water to do so. If the organic load is large, the oxygen is consumed faster than the atmosphere and the algae can
replace it, and the water goes anoxic. Aerobic organisms then die and anaerobic ones take over, producing ammonia
and sulphides — which is why a dead river smells of rotten eggs rather than of sewage. The deck gives the minimum
dissolved oxygen for fish life as 4 to 8 mg/l, and that range is the threshold the whole calculation is about.

**Definition**

[Biochemical oxygen demand](#gt-4-bod) is the mass of oxygen consumed by micro-organisms
while oxidising the biodegradable organic matter in a water sample under stated conditions, and it is therefore an
indirect measure of how much food the water is carrying. [Chemical oxygen demand](#gt-4-cod)
is the oxygen equivalent of everything in the sample that can be oxidised by a strong chemical oxidant, whether or
not bacteria can touch it.

The three numbers form a set. Dissolved oxygen is the state of the river; BOD is the demand the discharge places
on it, measured over a standard incubation because the rate of bacterial oxidation is slow and temperature-dependent;
COD is the total oxidisable load, obtained in hours rather than days. COD is always at least as large as BOD, and
the gap between them measures the non-biodegradable fraction. A discharge with a high COD but a low BOD contains
solvents, dyes or other resistant compounds that will pass through a biological treatment plant untouched — which
is exactly why both are measured and neither alone is sufficient.

**Exam pointer**Any question on water quality expects the triad to be defined functionally:
DO is what the fish breathe, BOD is what the bacteria will take from it, COD is what a chemist can oxidise. Say why
each is measured, and the difference between BOD and COD answers the biodegradability question for free.

* Point sources are piped and samplable; non-point sources arrive as runoff and are not.
* Organic load kills by removing oxygen, not by toxicity.
* Dissolved oxygen below about 4 mg/l is the threshold at which fish life fails.
* COD exceeds BOD; the difference is the non-biodegradable fraction.
* Thermal discharge stratifies the water body and accelerates oxygen consumption at the same time.

### 4.8Health hazards, effluent standards and treatment

Water-borne disease is the largest environmental health burden in India, and the engineer's answer to
it is a treatment train chosen to match the pollutant.

#### Water-borne disease

Consumption of polluted water is a major cause of ill health in India, and the deck's list is the one to
reproduce: cholera, dysentery, diarrhoea, tuberculosis and jaundice. The route is faecal-oral — pathogens from human
and animal excreta reach the water, the water is drunk, and the cycle closes. The deck's own figures are that
diarrhoea and pneumonia are the biggest killers of children under five in India, and that about 80 per cent of
stomach disease in the country is caused by polluted water. Sewage in a water body also poisons shellfish, which
then produce paralytic shellfish poisoning in the people who eat them — the toxin is concentrated by the filter
feeder and is not destroyed by cooking.

#### Heavy metals and toxic pollutants

Excess metals in drinking water cause general weakness, mental retardation, failure of internal organs, cancer and death. Each has its own target. Lead blocks haem synthesis and attacks the nervous system, giving anaemia plus cognitive loss, worst in children. Mercury is methylated in sediment, accumulates up the food chain and produces neurological damage. Cadmium concentrates in the kidney and softens bone. Hexavalent chromium, the deck's example from wet cement, is water-soluble, soaks through skin, causes allergic dermatitis and is a carcinogen by inhalation. Arsenic in groundwater produces skin lesions and, over long exposure, cancers. Manganese, zinc and copper are essential in trace quantity and toxic only in excess.

#### Eutrophication and biomagnification

[Eutrophication](#gt-4-eutrophication) is the enrichment of water with nutrients,
particularly nitrates and phosphates, that triggers excessive growth of algae. The sequence runs: sewage or
fertiliser runoff enters; nutrients accumulate; algae multiply into a bloom; the bloom shades itself and dies; the
decomposers that consume it multiply and use more oxygen; fish and other aerobic organisms die of oxygen shortage.
The bloom is the visible symptom and the oxygen crash is the mechanism, which is why the answer must include the
decomposer step and not stop at the green water.

[Biomagnification](#gt-4-biomagnification) is the accumulation of a non-biodegradable
substance at successively higher concentrations along a food chain. The deck's worked example is DDT: 0.2 ppm in
water, 77 ppm in algae, 500 to 600 ppm in fish, 1700 ppm in the pelican that eats the fish. DDT is not metabolised
and not excreted efficiently, so each trophic level retains what it eats, and the top consumer receives the sum. The
consequence in birds is thin eggshells and a falling population — a population effect produced by a concentration
that was never toxic in the water itself.

#### Effluent quality standards

An effluent standard is a limit placed on a listed parameter in a discharge, so that the receiving water can
still meet its designated use. The parameters are the ones this section has already defined: pH, because it governs
every other chemical behaviour and every biological process; biochemical oxygen demand, because it measures the
oxygen the discharge will steal; chemical oxygen demand, because it catches the non-biodegradable load that BOD
misses; total suspended solids, because they smother the bed and carry adsorbed pollutants; and oil and grease,
because they float, block light and hinder re-aeration.

| Parameter | Why it is limited | Value given in the deck |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH | Governs chemical and biological behaviour; extreme values are directly lethal | Natural fresh water quoted at 6 to 8 |
| Dissolved oxygen | The state of the receiving water, not of the effluent | 4 to 8 mg/l given as the minimum for fish life |
| BOD | Oxygen the discharge will remove from the river | No numeric limit stated in the deck |
| COD | Total oxidisable load, including non-biodegradable matter | No numeric limit stated in the deck |
| Total suspended solids | Smothering, turbidity, adsorbed pollutants | No numeric limit stated in the deck |
| Oil and grease | Floating film, light blockage, hindered re-aeration | No numeric limit stated in the deck |

**Source of the values.** The Module IV deck identifies the parameters that an effluent standard controls but
gives no numeric discharge limits for any of them, and no standard is attributed in the slides. The only quantitative figures it supplies are the natural pH range of 6 to 8 and the 4 to 8 mg/l of dissolved oxygen needed for fish. Limits for BOD, COD, suspended solids and oil and grease are set by the pollution control authorities for each industry and are not reproduced here, because inventing them would be worse than saying the notes lack them.

#### The treatment train

Treatment is staged because each stage removes what the previous one could not. **Primary** is physical: bar screens catch rags and plastics, a grit channel drops sand, and a primary settler lets settleable solids fall as sludge — perhaps half the suspended solids and little of the dissolved load. **Secondary** is biological: in activated sludge the sewage is aerated with a dense bacterial culture that oxidises the dissolved organic matter, and a secondary settler separates the biomass as humus sludge, part returned to the aeration tank and part wasted; a trickling filter does the same with a biofilm on a packed bed. **Tertiary** is whatever the receiving water demands — nutrient removal, filtration, adsorption. **Disinfection** by chlorine, ultraviolet or ozone is a separate final step, because a plant that removes BOD perfectly can still discharge viable organisms.

### 4.9Waste: identification, characterisation and classification

Waste management begins with knowing what the waste is: which stream it came from, what it contains,
and whether it is hazardous — because that answer fixes every step that follows.

**Definition**

Waste management is the collection, transport, recovery and disposal of waste, including the supervision of those
operations and the aftercare of disposal sites. The deck adopts the European Union Directive wording and then adds
the newer layer: reduce, reuse and recycle, over and above disposal.

Waste may be solid, liquid or gaseous; biodegradable or non-biodegradable; hazardous or non-hazardous. The
management system covers municipal, industrial, biological, household, organic, biomedical and radioactive waste,
and most of the operational burden in an Indian municipality is municipal solid waste. The effects that justify the
effort are environmental — surface water, groundwater, soil and air contamination — and economic, in municipal
wellbeing and in the revenue that recycling can generate.

#### Identification

Identification answers a deceptively simple question: what is in the bin. The practical tool is the waste audit,
which takes each process or department in turn, lists every material that leaves it, and measures the quantity by
weight or volume over a stated period. The audit produces a stream-by-stream inventory — paper, food, plastics,
glass, metal, textiles, construction debris, chemicals, batteries, discarded equipment — and a composition profile
that says what fraction of the total each represents. Without that profile, a recycling scheme is designed for the
waste the designer imagines rather than the waste the city produces.

#### Characterisation

Characterisation measures the properties that decide which treatment route is even possible. Physical
characterisation covers bulk density, moisture content, particle size, compaction behaviour and calorific value.
Chemical characterisation covers proximate analysis (moisture, volatile matter, fixed carbon, ash), ultimate
analysis, pH, heavy-metal content, chlorine and sulphur content, and the leaching potential of the material when it
is wetted. Biological characterisation covers biodegradability — the fraction that will decompose — and pathogen
content, which decides whether the material is a composting feedstock or a biohazard.

#### Classification

Classification sorts the inventory into categories that each carry their own legal route. By source: municipal,
industrial, commercial, institutional, construction and demolition, agricultural, biomedical and [electronic](#gt-4-e-waste). By
physical state: solid, liquid, sludge, gas. By hazard: non-hazardous, and hazardous, where hazardous is a defined
set of characteristics — ignitable, corrosive, reactive, toxic, infectious and radioactive. A waste is hazardous if
it exhibits any one of them, which is why a laboratory solvent and a discarded thermometer travel by the same route
although they are chemically unrelated.

| Stream | Typical source | Character | Route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Municipal solid waste | Households, markets, streets | Mixed, wet, biodegradable fraction high | Segregation, composting, recycling, landfill |
| Industrial waste | Manufacturing, processing | Variable; may be inert or hazardous | Reuse in process, treatment, secure landfill |
| Hazardous waste | Chemical plants, workshops, laboratories | Ignitable, corrosive, reactive or toxic | Licensed treatment, storage and disposal |
| Biomedical waste | Hospitals, clinics, laboratories | Infectious, sharps, pathological | Autoclave or incinerate; never mixed with municipal |
| Electronic waste | Discarded appliances and equipment | Metals, plastics, lead, mercury, brominated flame retardants | Dismantling and recovery by authorised recyclers |
| Construction debris | Demolition and site clearance | Inert, heavy, bulky | Crushing for aggregate; fill |

Segregation at source is the decision that fixes everything downstream, and it is worth stating as a rule rather
than a preference. A mixed stream cannot be composted, because the plastic and the batteries go into the windrow; it
cannot be recycled economically, because the cost of separating wet, contaminated material exceeds the value of
what is recovered; and it cannot be incinerated safely, because the chlorine and the heavy metals in the mixture
become dioxins and fly-ash contamination. Once the streams are mixed, every later stage is a more expensive attempt
to undo the mixing, and the residues of those attempts are what end up in the landfill.

**Exam pointer**A question on identification and characterisation wants the three levels
separated: identification is the audit and the inventory, characterisation is the physical, chemical and biological
testing, classification is the assignment to a stream and a hazard category. Give one example of each test and the
answer is complete.

* Waste management runs from generation to final disposal, including aftercare of the disposal site.
* Characterisation exists to choose a route: moisture and biodegradability point to composting, calorific value to incineration.
* Hazardous is a set of characteristics — ignitable, corrosive, reactive, toxic, infectious, radioactive.
* High chlorine means dioxins on incineration; high heavy metals mean leaching in a landfill.
* Segregation at source decides whether any downstream recovery is possible at all.

### 4.10Waste management: hierarchy, recycling and disposal

The waste hierarchy ranks the options by what they save, and every practical decision — compost or
incinerate, recycle or landfill — is a position on that ladder.

**Definition**

The [waste hierarchy](#gt-4-waste-hierarchy) is the ranking of waste management options in
order of preference: prevention first, then reuse, then recycling, then recovery of energy, and disposal last. The
order is not arbitrary — each step down the ladder consumes more energy and destroys more of the material's value
than the step above it.

#### The three R's in practice

**Reduce** is the only option that prevents the cost rather than managing it: double-sided copying, circulating documents instead of printing, durable cups, buying in bulk or with minimal packaging, reading online. **Reuse** keeps an object in service without reprocessing: envelopes and single-sided paper as scratch paper, packaging material, remanufactured equipment, rechargeable batteries and refillable cartridges, composted food waste, donated clothing. **Recycle** breaks the material down and makes it into something new, and the deck's benefits are the standard ones: it saves natural resources, prevents greenhouse gas and water pollutants, saves energy, supplies industry and creates jobs, and saves landfill space. Paper, glass, metal and plastic justify a municipal scheme; all four lose quality each cycle, so the loop leaks.

#### Collection, composting and biogas

Collection is the most visible and most expensive part of the system: segregated streams must be kept separate in
the vehicle as well as at the kerb, or the segregation is undone at the first transfer station. The wet, putrescible
fraction — the largest part of Indian municipal waste by weight — is best treated biologically. Aerobic composting
stacks it in windrows that are turned to keep oxygen in, and the product is a soil conditioner. Anaerobic digestion
does the same decomposition in a sealed reactor and produces biogas, mostly methane, which can be burned for cooking
or electricity, with a digestate that is also a fertiliser. The difference is that composting releases the carbon as
carbon dioxide and digestion captures it as fuel.

#### Incineration with energy recovery

Incineration cuts volume drastically and recovers calorific value, which places it at level four of the hierarchy rather than five. It does not destroy matter: heavy metals and chlorine leave in the flue gas and the fly ash, and incomplete combustion at the wrong temperature makes dioxins and furans. A modern plant therefore needs combustion control and a full air pollution control train, and its fly ash is itself a hazardous waste. Mixed Indian municipal waste is also wet and low in calorific value, which is the practical reason composting and recycling come first.

#### Sanitary landfill

A sanitary landfill is an engineered reactor, not a dump. The base carries compacted clay and a synthetic liner to keep the liquid percolating through the waste — the [leachate](#gt-4-leachate) — out of the groundwater; a drainage layer collects it and pipes it to treatment; gas wells vent or flare the methane from anaerobic decomposition, which is both a fire risk and a potent greenhouse gas; each day's waste is covered with soil; and the completed cell is capped, revegetated and monitored for decades while settlement and leachate production continue.

#### Extended producer responsibility

Extended producer responsibility places the cost of take-back and safe disposal on the producer rather than on the
municipality, on the argument that only the producer can design a product that is easy to dismantle and free of
hazardous components. It is the mechanism behind rules on electronic waste, batteries, plastic packaging and used
oil. For hazardous waste specifically, the deck's position is that recycling, reuse and reclamation are preferable
to disposal, with environmental benefits — lower raw-material consumption, less pollution, less energy, less water —
and economic ones, since recycling raises production efficiency, cuts raw-material cost, and can eliminate a waste
stream and with it the regulatory burden of disposing of it.

**Exam pointer**For a waste-management question, place every option on the ladder before
discussing it. A description of incineration that does not say it sits below recycling, or of landfill that does not
say it is the last resort, reads as a list of technologies rather than as management.

* Hierarchy order: prevention, reuse, recycling, energy recovery, disposal — value falls at each step down.
* Composting releases carbon; anaerobic digestion captures it as biogas.
* Incineration destroys volume, not matter: fly ash is hazardous and dioxins are a combustion control problem.
* A landfill needs liner, leachate collection and treatment, gas venting, daily cover and long aftercare.
* Extended producer responsibility moves take-back cost to the party that designs the product.

## Safe Working Environment, Welfare and PPE

Inspection, first aid, welfare facilities and personal protection — the systems that keep a site liveable.

**What the syllabus asks**Purpose and benefits of safety inspection; first-aid appliances; shelters, restrooms and lunch rooms; personal protective equipment; individual’s role in conserving natural resources; methods for controlling water pollution; individual’s role in preventing pollution.

### 5.1Safety inspection: purpose and benefits

An inspection is the cheapest instrument in occupational safety: it buys information about the workplace before the workplace charges a worker for it.

Every control in this course — a guard, a ventilation hood, a permit to work, a respirator — assumes somebody already knows where the hazard is and whether the control still works. Inspection produces that knowledge. It is not an audit of paperwork and not a fault-finding walk for managers; it is a structured examination of the physical workplace, on a schedule, recorded, closed out and repeated.

**Definition**

A [safety inspection](#gt-5-safety-inspection) is the process of critically examining the workplace to identify and investigate hazards, confirm that standards are met, and initiate action that reduces the severity of what is found, so that the workplace is in fact safe and free from unacceptable risk.

#### What an inspection is looking for

The deck reduces the target to four categories, and each fails differently.

* **Unsafe equipment** — guards removed, interlocks bypassed, cracked ladders, frayed slings. Equipment degrades silently and the machine runs on after the protection has gone.
* **Unsafe conditions** — the state of the place: blocked exits, oil on the floor, lighting failed over a stair, a duct re-routed so the hood no longer captures.
* **Unsafe processes** — a method that cannot be done safely as written: a two-person job given to one, isolation skipped because it takes an hour.
* **Unsafe practice** — what people actually do: shortcuts, cleaning a machine with compressed air, working at height from a forklift pallet.

#### The four areas and the purposes

A workplace is not only machinery, so the model scans four areas in parallel: **people** (workers, behaviour and methods), **plant** (machinery, equipment, vehicles), **premises** (the workplace and its environment) and **procedures** (the methodology and safe system of work). An inspection that only walks past machines covers one of the four.

Inspections exist so the organisation can listen to workers, understand the jobs, identify existing and potential hazards and their underlying causes, recommend corrective action, monitor what was already done, and meet regulatory and management-system requirements. An inspection record is often the only contemporaneous evidence that a hazard was known and managed.

#### Benefits

Two are human: regular checks give employees an increased sense of safety, and a visible regime signals that management takes safety seriously — which builds a high-performance safety culture. Two are financial: an inspector who notices a failing bearing converts a large repair into a small one, and preventing injury prevents the litigation, lost time and insurance cost that follow. Culture is the benefit examiners reward, because it keeps inspections working between inspections.

#### Types of inspection

| Type | Trigger and rhythm | Conducted by | Documentation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Informal | Continuous; part of the working day and of every worksite visit | Supervisors, safety officers with the area supervisor, employees in their own area | None required; findings raised at the safety meeting |
| Formal | Scheduled, planned walk-through of a facility or worksite | Safety officers, site or facility managers, area supervisors and employees | Mandatory: the specific hazard and the date it will be corrected |
| Special | Reactive: report of an unsafe act or condition, a new substance, process or equipment, or an accident investigation | Area or facility managers, health and safety department, with supervisors and employees | Mandatory: hazard, correction date, and follow-up |
| Statutory | Set by the law of the jurisdiction and at its intervals | The regulator's inspector, or the plant on the regulator's behalf | Prescribed by statute; non-compliance is an offence, not a finding |

The column that carries marks is the last. Informal inspection needs no paperwork; formal inspection must be documented, with the hazard described specifically and a correction date; special inspection must be documented *and* followed up, because a reactive inspection never closed out is how the same accident happens twice. A statutory inspection sits outside the hierarchy because its findings are enforceable rather than debatable.

#### How often, and what decides it

Frequency depends on the nature of the work, the materials and equipment involved and the procedures followed, which is why inspections may be daily, weekly, monthly or annually in different parts of one plant. The deciding factors are legislative requirements; past incident records; the number and size of operations; the type of equipment and processes; the number of shifts; new processes or machinery; and any high-hazard area, which earns extra attention. The schedule must state when each area is inspected, by whom, and to what depth: an inspection that does not specify its depth becomes a quick look, and inspections are ineffective when the time allowed permits only that.

#### The management-system context

OHSAS 18001, the Occupational Health and Safety Assessment Series, was an international standard for occupational health and safety management systems — a specification for how an organisation plans, implements, checks and improves, not for any guard or limit. Inspection is its "check" limb: it measures the plant against policy and legal duty and feeds the corrective loop back into planning. A certified plant is not one with fewer hazards; it is one that can prove it looks for them on a schedule and fixes what it finds. Standard reference: OHSAS 18001 has been superseded by ISO 45001, which keeps the same logic.

### 5.2Conducting an inspection

The difference between an inspection that changes a workplace and one that fills a file is preparation and follow-up; the walk itself is the short part.

#### Preparation

Planning sets the ceiling on what the inspection can find. The deck's questions are who, what, where, when and how, with two instructions that shape the route: attend to items likely to become unsafe through stress, wear, impact, vibration, heat or corrosion, and include areas where no work is done regularly — parking lots, rest areas, storage areas, locker rooms. Those are where a hazard sits unobserved for a year.

* **A diagram of the area.** From the plant layout, divide the workplace by process, mark machinery, equipment and materials, and show the movement of material and people, the air ducts, stairways, alarms and fire exits. The diagram stops the inspection becoming a tour of the places the inspector already knows.
* **An equipment inventory**, with the manufacturer's data sheets and safety manuals — often the only written statement of what a guard is supposed to do.
* **A hazardous product inventory.** Which products are used, whether safety data sheets exist, whether every source of exposure is controlled, and whether workers are trained and containers labelled.
* **Management documents** — policies, rules, procedures, safe work practices, records: the standard the inspection measures reality against.
* **Checklists and past records.** A [checklist](#gt-5-inspection-checklist) clarifies responsibilities, controls the activity and produces the report; but it is a prompt, not a boundary. Past records show what was found, what the earlier team concentrated on and — by omission — what it never examined; they are read to test whether recommendations were implemented, never copied forward.

Team selection follows the same logic: committee members where trained, chosen for knowledge of the regulations, the hazards and the work itself. Engineers, maintenance staff, hygienists and safety professionals join for the parts that need them. A team of managers inspecting a job none of them has done will find the housekeeping and miss the exposure.

#### The walk-through

Two rules come before the first step. The team discusses the planned route and what it is looking for, because a huddle before entering a noisy area removes the need for arm-waving and shouting — useless as communication and a hazard in itself. And the team wears [PPE](#gt-5-ppe) where required: without it the inspector does not enter, the absence is recorded as a deficiency, and the area is re-inspected once the PPE is provided. A team that exempts itself from the rules it enforces has lost the argument with the workforce.

The sequence: review the checklist first, work one room or floor at a time, take a supervisor who can answer questions, ask the employees who work there, and record who took part. The principles decide whether the observation is trustworthy. Deal with immediate danger at once — the rest can wait for the report. Shut down and lock out anything that cannot be brought to a safe standard. Do not operate equipment; ask for a demonstration, and treat an operator who does not know their machine's dangers as a finding. Never ignore an item because you feel unqualified — record and refer it. Look up, down, around and inside; the failure mode is the "once-over-lightly" approach. Record on the spot with the exact location, and note what you did and did not examine in case the inspection is interrupted. Judge both the static and the moving condition of what you are looking at. Discuss as a group whether the situation could generate an incident, how serious the outcome would be and which regulations apply. Photograph what you cannot describe.

The principle most often skipped is this: do not rely on your senses alone. Noise, chemical exposure, radiation and biological agents cannot be judged by eye or ear, and the inspection may have to request that equipment be monitored. An inspector who "can tell" the noise is fine has replaced a measurement with an impression.

#### Observing practice without policing it

Unsafe practice is recorded as deviation from accepted practice, factually: *a worker was observed operating a machine without a guard*. And here the deck states the rule that decides whether the system works — information from inspections is not used for disciplinary measures. If an inspection can get a colleague punished, workers stop being honest, hazards move out of sight, and the inspection measures appearance instead of condition. The practices to watch for: using machinery without authority; operating at unsafe speeds; removing guards; repairing equipment in motion, under pressure or live; using defective or overdue tools; using hands where a push stick belongs; improper lifting; incorrect storage — overloading, unbalanced stacks, incompatible items mixed, chemical cabinets unused; failing to maintain PPE; poor housekeeping and eating or smoking in unauthorised areas; cleaning skin with compressed air; standing under suspended loads or open hatches; and the psychosocial ones — workers overloaded, fatigued, in conflict, or working alone.

#### Recording, rating and reporting

The report carries five required contents: who conducted the inspection, with time, date and area; a clear description of each hazard; a priority ranking by risk; the temporary measures in place until a permanent solution; and recommendations for corrective action. In structure that is an introduction, numbered observations classified by hazard type and severity, a follow-up line naming the action, the responsible person and the due date, and distribution to the supervisors and the health and safety office.

The severity scheme is deliberately coarse, and coarse is right: **A, major or high**, immediate action; **B, serious or medium**, short-term action; **C, minor or low**, long-term action. The response rule gives the classification meaning: serious hazards are corrected immediately, non-serious as soon as possible, and where correction cannot be made in time the employer changes the procedure, takes the machine out of service, stops the operation, or takes whatever other action protects employees in the interim.

**Exam pointer**Sequence, not enthusiasm: prepare (information, team, checklist, route), inspect, record the specific hazard and its location, classify, assign owner and date, report, verify. The [hazard classification](#gt-5-hazard-classification) A/B/C and the interim measures separate a full-mark answer.

#### Why inspections become a paper exercise

Four failures account for most of it. The checklist is cloned year after year and describes a plant that no longer exists. Findings are written without an owner or a date, so nothing is anybody's job. Corrective actions are recorded but never verified, so the same item reappears and the workforce learns the report is theatre. And the inspection is used as a disciplinary instrument, so it stops seeing anything real. Where the whole workplace cannot be covered at once, rotate the section inspected each month, so every area is covered in depth rather than all in none.

* Preparation decides what can be found: diagram, inventories, SDS, procedures, checklist, past records, team.
* Senses are not instruments — noise, chemicals, radiation and bioaerosols must be measured.
* A/B/C severity, a named owner, a date, and a verification pass.

### 5.3First aid and first-aid appliances

First aid is the part of the safety system that assumes every other control has already failed at least once.

**Definition**

[First aid](#gt-5-first-aid) is the first and immediate assistance given to a person with a minor or serious illness or injury, with care provided to preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, or promote recovery until medical services arrive.

The three verbs are the whole scope. *Preserve life* covers airway, breathing, circulation and severe bleeding. *Prevent worsening* covers immobilising a suspected fracture, flushing a chemical from an eye, cooling a burn, and not moving a casualty with a possible spinal injury. *Promote recovery* covers comfort and positioning until the ambulance arrives. None of it is diagnosis or treatment.

#### Why it is a statutory duty

A factory is a place where injury is foreseeable, and the state cannot leave the response to goodwill, because goodwill may be absent on the shift where it is needed. Section 45 of the Factories Act, 1948 therefore makes first-aid provision an obligation of the occupier rather than an act of kindness. The deck's summary gives it four limbs.

1. The factory must provide and maintain proper first-aid boxes in every workroom. The number must not be less than one for every 100 or 50 workers — the deck states the ratio as that pair, so quote the range rather than picking a figure — and every box must hold the contents the Act prescribes.
2. Nothing except the prescribed contents may be kept in a first-aid box.
3. Each box must be under the control of a first-aid in-charge, who handles its requirements and its use and must be an expert in the field.
4. Where the number of workers exceeds 500, the factory must provide an [ambulance room](#gt-5-ambulance-room) with all necessary equipment.

A box that also holds spare fuses, a screwdriver and last month's tablets is not a first-aid box; it is a drawer, and contamination, expired dressings and a missing item found during an emergency follow from it. The fourth limb explains the escalation: the probability of a serious event rises with person-hours worked, and an ambulance room is the point at which the site must hold and stabilise a casualty rather than dress a cut.

#### What the box has to be able to do

Contents are prescribed by schedule, so describe the functions the stock serves rather than reciting a brand list.

| Function | Typical stock | Injury it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control bleeding and cover wounds | Sterile dressings, adhesive dressings, roller and triangular bandages, safety pins | Lacerations, punctures, crush injury |
| Clean and protect | Antiseptic, cotton wool, sterile gauze, adhesive tape | Abrasions, minor cuts, splinters |
| Flush | Eye wash, sterile water or saline, eye pads | Chemical splash, dust and foreign bodies |
| Dress a burn | Burn dressings, non-adherent pads | Thermal and chemical burns, metal spatter |
| Protect rescuer and casualty | Disposable gloves, scissors, forceps, blanket, mouth barrier | Blood-borne infection, chemical contamination |

The gloves and mouth barrier are not for the casualty: they stop a colleague acquiring a blood-borne infection while helping, which is the commonest way a first-aider becomes a patient. Eye wash must sit within reach of wherever chemicals are handled — the first half-minute decides how much cornea is burned.

#### Trained people, and the honest limit of the ratio

The Act as the deck presents it fixes the number of boxes and the qualification of the in-charge, not a headcount ratio of first-aiders to workers. Say so in an exam: quote the box ratio, the trained in-charge per box and the ambulance room above 500, and do not invent a first-aider figure the notes do not give. A plant needs more trained people than boxes, because a box is useless if its in-charge is on the other shift; every shift and every high-hazard area should have one trained person present, re-drilled on the site's own likely events.

#### Records

Two records make the system visible: the box's stock and check record, and the plant's treatment record of who was injured, how, what was done and whether they went to hospital. The first keeps the box stocked, because an item used on Tuesday is not there on Friday unless somebody wrote it down. The second turns first aid into prevention: a run of the same minor injury, on the same machine, at the same hour, is a hazard report nobody had to fill in.

#### The emergency sequence

1. **Make the scene safe** before approaching. The commonest way to double a casualty count is for the rescuer to walk into the same hazard — live conductor, unguarded machine, toxic atmosphere.
2. **Raise the alarm** and name the location precisely. A delayed call is the most common failure in factory emergencies.
3. **Assess** responsiveness, airway, breathing, circulation and catastrophic bleeding, in that order.
4. **Give first aid** within the limits of training: control bleeding, maintain the airway, position, immobilise, flush, cool.
5. **Hand over** a factual account — what happened, when, what was done, what the casualty has eaten or been given, and any chemical involved with its safety data sheet.
6. **Preserve the scene**, record the event, and investigate it. The scene is evidence, and it will be rearranged within minutes if nobody is told to leave it alone.

Two prohibitions belong with the sequence: do not move a casualty with a suspected spinal injury unless there is an immediate threat to life, and give nothing by mouth to a casualty who may need surgery.

#### The limits of first aid

First aid stabilises; it does not treat. Professionally, a first-aider acts within a defined syllabus. Legally, the standard is that of a reasonable person with that training, acting with consent where it can be given and in the casualty's best interest where it cannot. Practically, the damaging errors are not missed diagnoses but delays: treating when the ambulance should have been called, moving a casualty who should have been left still, looking for the box. Every requirement exists to shorten the interval between injury and competent care.

**Exam pointer**Section 45's four limbs in order — boxes at the prescribed ratio in every workroom, nothing but prescribed contents, a trained in-charge per box, an ambulance room above 500 — plus the definition's three verbs.

* Section 45: boxes at one per 100 or 50 workers, prescribed contents only, trained in-charge, ambulance room above 500.
* A box that holds anything else has stopped being a first-aid box.
* The notes fix the box ratio, not a first-aider-to-worker ratio; do not invent one.

### 5.4Welfare facilities

Welfare looks like comfort and behaves like engineering control: the washbasin, the shade and the lunch room are what stop a hazard entering the body.

In general terms welfare is the health, happiness and fortunes of a person or group; in the statutory sense it is the physical and material well-being an employer must provide as a condition of employing people at all. The Factories Act, 1948 treats it that way — duties about the body's ordinary needs, written into the same chapter as safety because a worker who cannot wash, sit, drink or eat properly is being injured slowly.

**Definition**

[Welfare facilities](#gt-5-welfare-facilities) are the statutory provisions an employer must make for the ordinary physical needs of workers at work — washing, clothing storage, seating, first aid, canteen, shelter, rest and lunch rooms — so that the working day can be spent without avoidable harm to health.

#### The provisions the deck cites

| Section | Subject | Applies when | Requirement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 42 | Washing facilities | Every factory | Adequate and suitable washing facilities for all workers; separate and adequately screened facilities for men and women; accessible to all. The State Government may prescribe standards for any factory, class or process. |
| 43 | Storing and drying clothes | Where workers do not wear their working clothes | The State Government may direct the place and the manner in which clothing is stored and dried. |
| 44 | Facilities for sitting | Every factory | Suitable seating so a worker can rest when free time allows, which also improves efficiency; the Chief Inspector may direct seating where a worker could work more efficiently sitting. |
| 45 | First-aid appliances | Every factory; ambulance room above 500 | Boxes at the prescribed ratio, prescribed contents only, a trained in-charge per box (§5.3). |
| 46 | Canteens | Above 250 workers | The State Government may require a canteen and set standards for construction, accommodation, furniture, equipment, the food served, the date by which it is provided, and a managing committee. |
| 47 | Shelters, rest-rooms and lunch-rooms | Above 150 workers | Shelters, rest-rooms and lunch-rooms provided and maintained, so workers can eat food brought from home. Where a lunch-room exists, no worker may eat in the work-room. |

Two features are examinable in themselves. The thresholds are population-based and escalate — 150 for shelter, rest and lunch rooms, 250 for a canteen, 500 for an ambulance room. And several sections give the State Government power to prescribe or direct, so an answer that says only "the factory shall provide" is incomplete: the Act sets the floor and delegates the detail.

Drinking water is the one welfare item the deck's section list does not number, so treat it as a requirement rather than quote a section for it: wholesome water at accessible points, vessels cleaned, supply protected from contamination. In a hot plant it is also the cheapest heat-strain control available.

#### Why welfare is a safety issue

The sections read like comfort; the physiology says otherwise. Five mechanisms connect them to injury.

**Heat and hydration.** A worker who cannot drink when thirsty loses concentration and reaction speed before any clinical sign appears, and heat strain ends in collapse. Shade and a rest room are what make it possible to cool down between exposures; a shelter is a heat control, not a perk.

**Fatigue.** Section 44 exists because capacity to stand has limits. Prolonged standing produces leg and back pain, and pain produces the short-cut — the load carried badly, the guard not replaced. Seating at the point of work is one of the cheapest error-reduction measures in a plant.

**Hygiene and absorption.** Washing facilities control a whole class of occupational dermatitis and percutaneous absorption: a worker handling solvents, pesticides or lead compounds with contaminated skin absorbs the substance through intact skin, and the washbasin is the barrier. Separate screened facilities for women are also an attendance issue.

**Ingestion.** Section 47's apparently trivial rule is an exposure control. Eating at the workstation means eating the dust — lead, silica, pesticide residue and metal fume on hands, food and table — and hand-to-mouth transfer is one of the best-documented routes of occupational lead intake. A lunch room with a washbasin before it breaks that route better than any respirator.

**Take-home contamination.** Section 43 extends the argument to the family: clothing worn home, shaken out at the door or washed with the children's clothes carries lead, asbestos fibres, pesticides and solvents into the house. Lockers, a change of clothes and separate laundering are the control.

Culture sits behind all five. A plant with blocked toilets, no shade and a lunch room used as a store returns unreported hazards and turnover.

#### Making welfare work in practice

Provision is not availability. Facilities must be reachable within the working time allowed — a washbasin at the far end of a plant is not a washing facility for the worker on the near side — clean, maintained, and separated from the process. Rest and lunch rooms need seating, ventilation and a washing point at the entrance, and canteens need a managing committee, which is why section 46 mentions one.

**Exam pointer**Two halves: sections 42 to 47 with their thresholds of 150, 250 and 500 and their delegations to the State Government, then the mechanisms — heat, fatigue, hygiene, ingestion, take-home contamination — with the no-eating rule explained as an exposure control.

* Thresholds escalate — shelter, rest and lunch rooms above 150; canteen above 250; ambulance room above 500.
* The Act sets the floor; the State Government and Chief Inspector set the standard and may direct.
* The lunch-room rule exists because eating at the workstation means ingesting the hazard.

### 5.5Personal protective equipment

PPE is the last line of defence: the only control that protects nobody unless the worker puts it on, and whose failure stays invisible until the injury.

The deck fixes PPE's place in the hierarchy before describing a single item: eliminate or substitute the toxic material, enclose or confine the operation, provide general or local exhaust ventilation, and only where engineering controls are not feasible fall back on protective equipment. An engineering control works on every worker, every shift, without being worn, remembered or fitted; PPE works on one worker for as long as they tolerate it. Everything below about selection, fit and care is an attempt to make the weakest control behave like a strong one.

**Definition**

[Personal protective equipment](#gt-5-ppe) is equipment worn or held by the worker that protects against one or more hazards to health and safety — head, eyes and face, respiratory system, hearing, hands, feet and legs, and the body — used where the hazard cannot be eliminated or controlled at source.

Figure — The six groups of PPE, head to toe. Each is selected for a named hazard; none substitutes for a control at source.

#### Head protection

Head injuries come from three directions: objects falling from above, the head striking a fixed object such as an exposed pipe or beam, and accidental contact with electrical hazards. The [classes of hard hat](#gt-5-hard-hat-classes) answer them differently.

| Class | Designed for | Electrical protection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G — General | Impact and penetration | Low voltage, proof-tested to 2,200 volts |
| E — Electrical | Electrical and utility work; falling objects and impact | High voltage, proof-tested to 20,000 volts |
| C — Conductive | Comfort; bumping against fixed objects | None — no protection from falling objects or electrical hazards |

Class C is the trap. It looks like a hard hat and is sold as one, but a conductive shell on a site with overhead work or live equipment is the wrong hat, not a cheaper one. A shell also loses its dielectric strength when drilled, painted or cracked, which is why damaged shells are replaced.

#### Eye and face protection

The causes are specific: chemical splashes, blood or other infectious material, intense light, dust and flying particles, and molten metal splashes. Selection follows five criteria — protect against the actual hazard, fit properly, allow unrestricted vision and movement, be durable and cleanable, and allow the other PPE to function. Where the worker wears corrective lenses the protection must incorporate the prescription or fit over it without disturbing its position; and a respirator whose straps lift safety glasses off the eyes produces a worker who removes one of the two.

| Device | Protects against | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safety glasses | Moderate impact from particles | No seal, so no protection against splash, dust or vapour |
| Goggles | Impact, dust, splashes to the eye and its surrounding area | The vent type decides whether it stops splash |
| Face shield | Nuisance dusts, splashes and sprays of hazardous liquid | Face protection, not eye protection; no impact rating unless stated. Wear glasses or goggles beneath |
| Welding shield | Burns from infrared and intense radiant light; sparks, spatter, slag chips | Shade must match the process; not general-purpose eye protection |
| Laser goggles | Flying particles, ultraviolet, laser and welding radiation | Wavelength-specific; the wrong pair invites exposure |

The goggle types trade fogging against splash. Direct-ventilated goggles circulate air and resist fogging but stop only large particles; indirect-ventilated goggles pass air through a baffle, so they resist fogging and still block splash; non-ventilated goggles admit no air, stop splash and fog, and the worker who takes them off to see is unprotected.

#### Respiratory protection

Respirators divide by where the air comes from: an [air-purifying respirator](#gt-5-air-purifying-respirator) filters what it draws through, while an [atmosphere-supplying respirator](#gt-5-atmosphere-supplying-respirator) brings its own air.

| Family | Types | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air-purifying (APR) | Particulate respirator; chemical cartridge or gas mask; powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) | Removes contaminants from the air it draws through the filter. Useless in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, against a contaminant the cartridge is not rated for, or where concentration is unknown |
| Atmosphere-supplying | Self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA); supplied-air respirator (SAR) | Supplies clean, breathable air from a cylinder or line, independent of the surrounding atmosphere |

Before fit testing and authorisation, a medical evaluation must determine the employee's ability to use a respirator, done by a physician or other licensed health care professional. This is not bureaucracy: a tight-fitting respirator adds breathing resistance and dead space, and a worker who cannot tolerate it will fail at the wrong moment or quietly loosen the straps. Care follows the same logic — inspect before and after every use, wash in detergent, disinfect by immersion in a sanitising solution, dry, and store so the facepiece keeps its shape, away from dust, sunlight, heat, cold, moisture and chemicals.

#### Hearing protection

Exposure above 85 dB can cause hearing loss, protection is required at 90 dB, and impact noise should not exceed 140 dB. The permitted duration falls as level rises: 90 dBA for 8 hours, 92 for 6, 95 for 4, 97 for 3, 100 for 2, 105 for 1, 110 for half an hour and 115 for a quarter hour, under the 29 CFR 1910.95(b)(1) relation the deck reproduces. The employer must provide ear protection wherever the level exceeds that table.

Devices are foam plugs, moulded plugs, noise-cancelling plugs and muffs, compared by [noise reduction rating](#gt-5-noise-reduction-rating). The rating is a laboratory number and the field number is always worse: a foam plug inserted shallowly loses most of its attenuation, and muffs over spectacle arms break their seal at the temple. That is why the [hearing conservation programme](#gt-5-hearing-conservation-programme) matters more than the device — audiometry finds the loss before the worker does, and training fixes insertion.

#### Hand, foot, leg and body

Hand hazards are skin absorption, lacerations and severe cuts, punctures, chemical burns, thermal burns and extreme temperatures. Foot hazards are falling or rolling heavy objects, crushing or penetrating materials, sharp objects through the sole, molten metal, hot, wet or slippery surfaces and electrical contact. Glove selection starts at the safety data sheet, not at the colour of the box: a glove that swells, softens or lets a solvent through in minutes is worse than a bare hand, because it holds the chemical against the skin. Body protection runs from laboratory coats and coveralls to surgical gowns and full-body suits.

| Group | Device | Answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand | Gloves specified by the SDS for the chemical | Absorption, cuts, punctures, chemical and thermal burns |
| Foot and leg | Steel or composite toe-cap; metatarsal guard; metal shank; heat-resistant sole | Impact, compression, puncture through the sole, molten metal, heat |
| Foot, electrical | Conductive or nonconductive footwear, chosen for the task | Static build-up on one site, step and touch potential on the other |
| Body | Paper-like fibre; treated wool and cotton; duck; leather; rubber, neoprene or plastics | Dust and splash; fire, abrasion; cuts; dry heat and flames; certain chemicals |

**Exam pointer**Eight marks want the hierarchy first, then the six groups with a hazard and a device each, then the limitations: it protects only the wearer, only while worn, only if fitted and maintained, and it adds heat, weight and restricted vision and hearing. No Module V question appears in the three past papers in the folder, so the long questions at the end of this module are drawn from the syllabus wording.

#### Why PPE fails

Selection, fit, inspection, care, storage and training are the six stages at which PPE is lost. The wrong device is chosen for the hazard. It does not fit — one size does not cover a workforce, and an unfitted respirator leaks at the face seal. It is uncomfortable, so it is worn on the forehead or removed for "just a minute", which is the minute the exposure happens in. It is incompatible with the other PPE the task needs. It degrades in storage or sunlight, or a disposable is reused until it is a rag. It is issued without training, so nobody checks the seal. The deepest failure is cultural: where PPE is the only control, any lapse in wearing it is an exposure, not a near miss.

* PPE is last: eliminate, substitute, enclose, ventilate, then protect the person.
* Hard hats — G to 2,200 V, E to 20,000 V, C conductive and bump-only.
* Air-purifying respirators filter the atmosphere; atmosphere-supplying ones replace it.
* 85 dB is where loss begins, 90 dB is where protection is required, 140 dB is the impact ceiling.

### 5.6The individual's role in conserving natural resources

Conservation is not a separate activity from safety: both are about not spending a resource faster than it can be replaced, and the individual controls the spending at the point of use.

Resource conservation has three moves — use less, use it again, use it better — and the deck applies all three to water, energy, minerals, food and forests. Individual action matters because demand is the sum of many small decisions, and because the person at the tap, the switch and the shop counter is the only one who can change them without waiting for a policy.

#### Water

Check for leaks and repair them; a dripping tap is a continuous loss that no tariff will make visible. Adopt minimum-use patterns rather than convenience ones. Install water-saving toilets that use the optimum volume per flush. Collect wastewater in the home and use it on the kitchen garden, which closes the loop between what the house discharges and what the garden demands. Cooperate with beach clean-ups in coastal areas, and protect wetlands, lakes, ponds and wells, because a wetland is a treatment plant that costs nothing to run and cannot be rebuilt once it is filled.

#### Energy

Construct buildings so that the maximum amount of sunlight is obtained. Dry clothes in sunlight rather than in a machine drier. Use solar cookers for cooking, and buy energy-efficient appliances after checking the consumption figure rather than the purchase price. Minimise the use of automobiles by cycling, using public transport and carpooling; live near the place of work where that is possible; keep vehicles tuned for low fuel consumption. Wear adequate woollen clothes in winter instead of running a heat convector. Plant deciduous trees in the right place outside the house: they cut the intense summer heat, cut electricity consumption and provide a cool breeze, while letting winter sun through.

#### Materials, food and forests

Minimise the use of minerals likely to be depleted, recycle and reuse minerals and glass, buy durable products, repair and reuse bicycles, and use recyclable utensils. On food, the deck's measures are sustainable use and no waste: eat only what is needed, buy local and seasonal vegetables to save the energy of transport, storage and preservation, prefer organic food, and discourage packed, canned and preserved food. On forests: do not uproot existing trees when building, plant fast-growing species and suitable shrubs or trees around the home, join community plantation programmes, cooperate with NGOs that save trees, and tag a plantation to birthdays and anniversaries so the trees are looked after rather than merely planted.

Soil and biodiversity are the resources that disappear without a bill. Compost garden and kitchen waste rather than burning it, so organic matter and its nutrients return to the soil; never pour oils, paints or solvents onto the ground; keep ground cover and plant natives, because bare soil erodes and a mown monoculture supports almost nothing. On a construction site the same principle scales up: strip and stockpile topsoil for reuse, and control silt before it leaves the boundary.

The honest limit of this list is worth stating. Individual restraint reduces demand, but it does not substitute for regulation, and a household that sorts its waste while an industry discharges untreated effluent has not solved the problem. The individual's real leverage is where they hold a decision — a purchase, a habit, a vote, or a specification at work — and the largest of those is usually the workplace, where one person's insistence on a less toxic solvent or a closed loop can outweigh a decade of domestic thrift.

**Exam pointer**Breadth, not depth: group as water, energy, materials and minerals, food, and forests, and give two actions with the mechanism for each. The mechanism is what separates a list from an answer.

* Three moves: use less, use again, use better.
* Water — repair leaks, low-flush fixtures, greywater to the garden, protect wetlands.
* Energy — solar and daylight, public transport, tuned vehicles, deciduous shade.

#### Source control and ecosystem stabilisation

The deck opens with [stabilisation of the ecosystem](#gt-5-ecosystem-stabilisation), which is a set of practices rather than a plant: reduction of waste at source, harvesting and removal of biomass, trapping of the nutrients, and fish management. The logic is that a water body has a finite capacity to absorb nutrients, and that capacity is restored by removing the biomass that has taken them up and by preventing more from arriving. Water hyacinth is used in the same spirit — it is extremely efficient at absorbing and concentrating dissolved nutrients from the water it grows in, so a hyacinth pond becomes a nutrient pump that can be harvested; the harvested plant must then be removed, or the nutrients return when it decays.

#### Chemical and physical methods

| Method | What it does | Where it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Ion exchange](#gt-5-ion-exchange) | Reversible exchange of an ion in solution for a similarly charged ion held on an immobile solid — natural zeolite or synthetic resin | Removing dissolved ionic contaminants; softening; polishing |
| [Reverse osmosis](#gt-5-reverse-osmosis) | Purification through a semipermeable membrane; removes bacteria, organics and silica and reduces dissolved salt content by more than 95% | Pre-treatment for deionisation; high-salinity effluent |
| Precipitation | Transforms dissolved contaminants into insoluble solids, removed by sedimentation or filtration | Heavy metals and other dissolved species |
| Coagulation | Destabilises and chemically precipitates colloidal particles, accumulating them into larger flocs removed by gravity settling or filtration | Colloidal and suspended solids, turbidity, colour |

Thermal pollution is handled separately, by cooling the effluent before discharge — cooling ponds, wet and dry cooling towers, evaporation towers. The choice between wet and dry turns on water availability against cooling efficiency, which is the same trade a power station makes in its main condenser.

#### Reuse and treatment of the waste stream

The deck's [4R concept](#gt-5-four-r-concept) — recycling, renovation, recharge and reuse — treats wastewater as a resource: domestic sewage, industrial effluent and thermal and radioactive wastes receive some treatment before mixing into water bodies, and urban sewage and sludge may be recycled and reused to generate cheaper fuel, gas and electricity. Sanitation and sewage treatment is the largest single lever: municipal wastewater can be handled by centralised sewage treatment plants, decentralised systems, nature-based solutions or onsite septic tanks, and well-designed systems with secondary or more advanced tertiary stages can remove 90 per cent or more of the pollutant load. Safely managed sanitation also prevents the pollution that comes from having no sanitation at all. Industrial wastewater is treated to a standard that allows reuse, discharge to a sanitary sewer, or release to surface water; agricultural wastewater — from confined animal operations and from runoff contaminated by fertiliser, pesticide, slurry, crop residue or irrigation water — is handled as a farm management agenda, often in mechanised units similar to industrial plant.

Two further controls complete the chain. Erosion and sediment control on construction sites uses mulching and hydroseeding to hold the soil, and sediment basins and silt fences to catch what still moves; toxic discharges such as motor fuel and concrete washout are prevented by spill prevention and control plans, purpose-designed containers and diversion berms. Urban runoff is controlled by slowing the stormwater and reducing its peak flow, since a slower, smaller peak carries less sediment. Rainwater harvesting belongs at the same point in the chain: roof runoff collected for recharge or non-potable use never becomes polluted surface water at all.

Legislation and enforcement supply the pressure that makes the chain operate: discharge standards, consent to discharge, monitoring and penalty. The technical methods exist; what determines whether they are used is whether the cost of not using them is real.

**Exam pointer**Answer as a chain, not a list: source reduction and ecosystem stabilisation, then ion exchange, reverse osmosis, precipitation and coagulation, then cooling, then the 4R concept and sewage treatment, then erosion, sediment and runoff control, then legislation.

* Ecosystem stabilisation — waste reduction at source, biomass harvesting, nutrient trapping, fish management.
* Ion exchange, reverse osmosis, precipitation and coagulation are the four chemical methods the deck names.
* Secondary or tertiary sewage treatment removes 90 per cent or more of the pollutant load.

### 5.8The individual's role in preventing pollution

Most pollution is decided long before it reaches a chimney or a drain: it is decided in what is bought, how it is used, and what is done with the remains.

The deck's account of the individual's role runs from direct actions — what a person does with a battery, a bag, a vehicle — to indirect ones, where the same person spreads awareness, supports environmental education and helps enforce the law by respecting it. Both matter, and the indirect role is the one students forget.

#### Waste, water and energy at home

Divide domestic waste into biodegradable and non-biodegradable components before it leaves the house. Biodegradable waste becomes compost or fertiliser; non-biodegradable waste is collected for recycling; domestic waste is never scattered. Recharge batteries instead of using disposable ones. Use cloth bags rather than plastic, and carry a handkerchief rather than paper tissues. Buy environmentally friendly electronics, and recycle and reuse rather than sending material to landfill. Dispose of cells, batteries and pesticide containers properly, because these are the fractions that leach. Use water efficiently, use the minimum amount of detergent, and use only phosphate-free soaps and detergents — phosphate is the nutrient that turns a river green. Use renewable resources by installing solar heaters and using solar cookers, and reduce the use of refrigerators and air conditioners where possible, since refrigerants are the main source of the CFCs responsible for ozone depletion.

#### Transport and air

Walk or cycle instead of taking a vehicle, especially for short distances; use mass transport — buses and trains — instead of a personal vehicle; pool with colleagues from nearby localities when travelling to work. Take a personal vehicle for periodic pollution checks at centres approved by the authorities, keep filters clean, use quality fuel, switch the engine off while standing at a crossing, and use an efficient silencer and a low-noise horn. CNG is preferred over liquid petroleum for auto engines. Burning plastics, firecrackers and household waste is a direct contribution to air pollution, and so is smoking; LPG for cooking replaces wood and coal, which are far worse at the point of use.

#### At work, and beyond it

| Role | What the individual actually does | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct — process | Report leaks, spills and unnecessary flaring; use the closed container; do not rinse residues to the drain | Most fugitive pollution is visible to the operator first |
| Direct — consumption | Segregate waste at the bin, not at the dump; return solvent and used oil to the collection point | Segregation is the only step that makes recycling possible |
| Indirect — influence | Raise the substitution question in a toolbox talk or a committee; specify a less toxic product | One specification change outlives years of individual thrift |
| Indirect — education | Publish information, organise exhibitions and competitions, sponsor environment-related advertising, support NGOs, celebrate environmental dates such as Tree Plantation Day on 7 July and World Environment Day | Enforcement needs a public that understands the rule |

The deck also asks individuals to organise clean-up drives for streets and drains with neighbours. The point is the same: the individual's leverage multiplies when the action is copied.

**Exam pointer**Separate the direct and indirect roles, give one action per medium — air, water, solid waste, noise — then one sentence on why individual action supports but does not replace regulation.

* Segregate at source; biodegradable to compost, non-biodegradable to recycling, never scattered.
* Transport: walk, cycle, public transport, carpool, tuned engine, pollution check.
* At work the largest lever is participation in the management system, not private virtue.

#### Module 1 · Introduction to Occupational Health and Toxicology

Asbestosis
:   Diffuse interstitial pulmonary fibrosis caused by retained asbestos fibres, worst at the lung bases and subpleural regions. It is one of several effects of the fibre, alongside pleural plaques, lung cancer and mesothelioma.

    used in [§1.6](#s1-6)

Carcinogen
:   An agent capable of causing cancer in living tissue. Genotoxic carcinogens react with DNA and initiate the change, so no threshold is assumed; non-genotoxic agents promote division or suppress repair, and a threshold may exist for them. Effects appear after years or decades.

    used in [§1.6](#s1-6)

Duty of care
:   The common law obligation on an employer to take reasonable care of the safety of employees. It is open-ended rather than a fixed list, and it tightens as knowledge of a hazard improves, so statutory compliance alone does not discharge it.

    used in [§1.1](#s1-1)

Hierarchy of controls
:   The ranked order in which controls are applied: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment. Effectiveness falls down the list as dependence on individual behaviour rises.

    used in [§1.8](#s1-8)

Industrial hygiene
:   The art and science of the anticipation, recognition, evaluation, communication and control of workplace environmental parameters that may cause injury, illness or impairment, covering biological, chemical, physical, ergonomic and psycho-social agents.

    used in [§1.7](#s1-7)

LD50
:   The dose at which death occurs in 50 per cent of an exposed population. It is the standard single number for comparing acute lethality, and it says nothing about chronic effects, carcinogenicity or sensitisation.

    used in [§1.7](#s1-7)

Musculoskeletal disorder (MSD)
:   A condition affecting the muscles, bones and joints, including tendinitis, carpal tunnel syndrome, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, fibromyalgia and bone fractures. Occupational cases are produced by sustained posture, repetition, heavy lifting and work at the end of the joint's range.

    used in [§1.4](#s1-4)

Noise-induced hearing loss (industrial deafness)
:   Hearing loss caused by exposure to high sound levels at work, presenting as temporary loss, permanent loss or tinnitus. Outer hair cells in the cochlea are damaged mechanically and metabolically and do not regenerate.

    used in [§1.5](#s1-5)

Occupational disease
:   Any disease that occurs primarily as a result of exposure to risk factors arising from work activity, counted as occupational when it is more prevalent in a given group of workers than in the population at large.

    used in [§1.2](#s1-2)

Occupational health
:   The ILO/WHO definition: the promotion and maintenance of the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being of workers in all occupations; prevention of departures from health caused by working conditions; protection from risks arising from factors adverse to health; and the adaptation of work to man and of each man to his job.

    used in [§1.1](#s1-1)

Pneumoconiosis
:   The general term for lung disease produced by the accumulation of inhaled mineral dust and the tissue reaction to it. Its named forms include silicosis, asbestosis, coal workers' pneumoconiosis and byssinosis.

    used in [§1.6](#s1-6)

Repetitive strain injury (RSI)
:   The umbrella term for upper-limb disorders caused by repeated forceful movement at speed — tenosynovitis, epicondylitis and carpal tunnel syndrome, in which the median nerve is compressed by swollen flexor tendon sheaths.

    used in [§1.4](#s1-4)

Silicosis
:   A fibrosing lung disease caused by inhaling respirable crystalline silica. Alveolar macrophages engulf the particle, cannot digest it, die and release it, so inflammation repeats and concentric hyaline nodules form, first in the upper lobes, with an excess risk of tuberculosis.

    used in [§1.6](#s1-6)

Threshold Limit Value (TLV)
:   The average concentration of an airborne substance to which most workers could be safely exposed over an eight-hour working day or forty-hour working week throughout a working lifetime. The related MAC is the peak concentration to which most workers could safely be exposed.

    used in [§1.7](#s1-7)

Tinnitus
:   The perception of ringing, whistling, hissing or buzzing in one or both ears without an external source. It commonly accompanies noise-induced hearing loss and cannot be cured; hearing aids and relaxation strategies give partial relief.

    used in [§1.5](#s1-5)

Work-related disease
:   A disease with multiple causes, in which factors in the work environment play a role together with other risk factors. Because work is one contributor among several, attribution and compensation are harder than for an occupational disease.

    used in [§1.2](#s1-2)

#### Module 2 · Chemical and Biological Hazards, and Noise

Aerosol
:   A relatively stable suspension of solid particles in air, liquid droplets in air, or solid particles dissolved or suspended in liquid droplets. Dust, fume, mist and fog are aerosols; a gas or a vapour is not.

    used in [§2.1](#s2-1)

Biohazardous agent
:   A biological agent — a micro-organism, cell culture or human endoparasite, including genetically modified forms — that may be able to provoke infection, allergy or toxicity in those exposed.

    used in [§2.4](#s2-4)

Biological safety cabinet
:   An enclosed ventilated workstation with inward airflow and HEPA filtration. Class I protects the operator, Class II protects operator and product and is standard for group 2 and 3 work, Class III is a gas-tight glove box for group 4 agents.

    used in [§2.7](#s2-7)

Containment
:   The measures that keep a biological agent where it was generated. Primary containment — cabinet, sealed cup, technique — protects the worker and the immediate environment; secondary containment — room, airflow, access control, waste route — protects the outside world.

    used in [§2.4](#s2-4)

Daily noise exposure level (LEX,8h)
:   The time-weighted average sound level over a nominal eight-hour working day. It is the risk predictor for hearing damage, because damage depends on the total energy — level multiplied by duration.

    used in [§2.8](#s2-8)

Decibel
:   A logarithmic unit of sound pressure level. An increase of 3 dB doubles the acoustic power and about 10 dB is heard as twice as loud, so small numerical differences are large changes in energy.

    used in [§2.8](#s2-8)

Exchange rate
:   The number of decibels by which the permitted exposure time is halved. At the 5 dB rate, 90 dB(A) is permitted for eight hours, 95 dB(A) for four and 100 dB(A) for two; NIOSH recommends 3 dB.

    used in [§2.8](#s2-8)

Fibre
:   A solid particle with a high aspect ratio of length to width. Because drag depends mainly on diameter, a long fibre can have the aerodynamic size of a small particle, reach the alveoli and resist engulfment by macrophages.

    used in [§2.1](#s2-1)

Fume
:   Solid particles formed by combustion, sublimation or condensation of a vaporised material — typically sub-micron metal oxides from welding, soldering or hot cutting. Distinguish it from dust, which is mechanically broken rather than condensed.

    used in [§2.1](#s2-1)

Local exhaust ventilation
:   Capture of a contaminant at the point of generation by a hood, duct, fan and filter. Its effectiveness depends on capture velocity, which falls sharply as the hood is moved away from the source.

    used in [§2.3](#s2-3)

Respirable dust
:   The fraction of airborne particles below about 5 µm aerodynamic diameter that penetrates to the alveoli, where there is no mucociliary escalator to clear it.

    used in [§2.1](#s2-1)

Risk group
:   One of four classes into which biological agents are placed according to pathogenicity to humans, hazard to the worker, transmissibility to the community and the availability of effective prophylaxis or treatment. Group 1 is unlikely to cause human disease; group 4 causes severe disease with a high spread risk and no effective prophylaxis.

    used in [§2.4](#s2-4)

Route of entry
:   The path by which a hazardous substance crosses the body's outer boundary: inhalation, skin absorption, ingestion or injection. The route fixes how much of the dose reaches the blood, and how fast.

    used in [§2.2](#s2-2)

Safety data sheet
:   A technical document supplied by the manufacturer of a material, giving its health effects, the hazard evaluation for handling, storage and use, the measures needed to protect workers, and the emergency procedures, in a fixed sixteen-section order.

    used in [§2.3](#s2-3)

Systemic effect
:   Injury at a site distant from the point of contact, produced after the substance has been absorbed and distributed by the blood — as against a local effect at the site of contact itself.

    used in [§2.2](#s2-2)

Temporary threshold shift
:   A temporary loss of hearing after exposure to loud noise, recovering within 16 to 48 hours — auditory fatigue. Repeated shifts are the warning that a permanent threshold shift is accumulating.

    used in [§2.8](#s2-8)

Vapour
:   The gaseous phase of a material that is ordinarily solid or liquid at room temperature and pressure, generated at a rate set by vapour pressure and temperature.

    used in [§2.1](#s2-1)

Zoonosis
:   An infection transmitted from vertebrate animals to humans, such as anthrax, leptospirosis, psittacosis or rabies; it explains why animal handlers, farmers, abattoir and veterinary staff carry risks a factory of the same size does not.

    used in [§2.6](#s2-6)

#### Module 3 · Safety in the Construction Industry

Absorbed dose
:   The energy absorbed from radiation per unit mass of material — measured in rad, 100 ergs per gram, or in gray, one joule per kilogram, with 1 Gy equal to 100 rad.

    used in [§3.9](#s3-9)

Arc flash
:   The sudden release of electrical energy through air across a high-voltage gap, giving thermal radiation and intense light at temperatures as high as 35,000°F, together with an arc blast pressure wave from rapidly heated air.

    used in [§3.7](#s3-7)

Battering
:   Cutting an excavation face back to a slope at which the soil will stand on its own, used where there is room at the top of the cut; the alternative to shoring where space allows.

    used in [§3.5](#s3-5)

Earthing
:   A conductive connection to the earth that eliminates unwanted voltage and gives leakage current a low-resistance path back to the source, so that the protective device operates and the casing does not sit at line potential.

    used in [§3.8](#s3-8)

Ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI)
:   A device that detects current returning by an unintended path to earth and interrupts the circuit, limiting the duration of a shock; the three types are the receptacle, the temporary or portable, and the circuit-breaker GFCI.

    used in [§3.8](#s3-8)

Guard rail
:   The top and mid rails fixed at every exposed edge of a platform, at heights that stop a person falling through or over the edge; with the toe board it forms the edge protection.

    used in [§3.3](#s3-3)

Ionizing radiation
:   Electromagnetic or particulate radiation with enough energy to produce ion pairs by interaction with matter, removing electrons from the atoms it passes through and leaving ions and free radicals.

    used in [§3.9](#s3-9)

Let-go current
:   The current at which involuntary contraction of the flexor muscles prevents the victim releasing the conductor; in the deck's table this freezing range is 6–25 mA for women and 9–30 mA for men.

    used in [§3.7](#s3-7)

Putlog
:   A scaffold tube with one flattened end built into the brickwork, so that the wall carries the inner end of the platform; characteristic of the putlog scaffold, which has a single row of standards.

    used in [§3.3](#s3-3)

Scaffold
:   A temporary structure erected to support access or working platforms, formed from individual tubes and couplers or from proprietary components, used where work cannot be done at ground level or from a finished floor.

    used in [§3.3](#s3-3)

Shoring
:   Supporting an excavation face with sheeting held by walers and struts, so that the soil is held up rather than relied upon to stand by itself.

    used in [§3.5](#s3-5)

Sievert
:   The SI unit of equivalent dose, obtained by multiplying the absorbed dose in rads by a quality factor for the radiation; 1 sievert equals 100 rem.

    used in [§3.9](#s3-9)

Step potential
:   The voltage difference between a person's two feet when fault current spreads outward through the ground, so that a person standing with the feet apart completes a circuit without touching anything live.

    used in [§3.7](#s3-7)

Toe board
:   A low board at the platform edge that prevents tools, materials and debris being kicked or blown off onto the people working below.

    used in [§3.3](#s3-3)

Transuranic waste
:   Waste containing transuranic alpha-emitting radionuclides with half-lives longer than 20 years; the alpha emission makes it dangerous if dispersed as dust and inhaled, and the long half-life demands geological rather than engineering isolation.

    used in [§3.10](#s3-10)

Trench box
:   A prefabricated steel box lowered into a trench to protect the operative working inside it while the ground outside is free to collapse; it must match the trench dimensions and be placed by plant.

    used in [§3.5](#s3-5)

Vitrification
:   Immobilising high level liquid waste in inert borosilicate glass, so that the radionuclides are locked into a leach-resistant solid for interim storage and eventual geological disposal.

Working platform
:   Any surface from which work can be carried out, or which serves as a means of access to a place of work — including a scaffold, a suspended scaffold, a cradle, a roof, a floor, a mobile elevating work platform, a step ladder and a stairway.

#### Module 4 · Air and Water Pollution, Clean Coal and Waste

Acid deposition
:   The transfer of acidic compounds from the atmosphere to the ground, as wet deposition in rain, snow, fog or mist or as dry deposition of the gases and particles themselves; sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides oxidise to sulphuric and nitric acid.

    used in [§4.3](#s4-3)

Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)
:   The mass of oxygen consumed by micro-organisms while oxidising the biodegradable organic matter in a water sample under stated conditions; an indirect measure of the food load the water carries, and therefore of the oxygen it will remove from a river.

    used in [§4.7](#s4-7)

Biomagnification
:   The increase in concentration of a non-biodegradable substance at each successive trophic level, because it is neither metabolised nor excreted; the deck's example runs from 0.2 ppm of DDT in water to 1700 ppm in a pelican.

    used in [§4.8](#s4-8)

Chemical oxygen demand (COD)
:   The oxygen equivalent of everything in a water sample that can be oxidised by a strong chemical oxidant, whether or not bacteria can attack it; always at least as large as BOD, and the gap between them measures the non-biodegradable fraction.

    used in [§4.7](#s4-7)

Chlorosis
:   Loss or bleaching of chlorophyll in a leaf, so that it turns pale yellow-green; photosynthesis falls because the pigment that captures light is depleted, and the plant loses yield even when the injury is not yet visible.

    used in [§4.3](#s4-3)

Clean coal technology
:   Any technology designed to enhance the efficiency of conversion and the environmental acceptability of coal extraction, preparation and use; the term covers washing, combustion improvement, flue-gas treatment, gasification and carbon capture, not a cleaned fuel.

    used in [§4.6](#s4-6)

Cyclone separator
:   A dry inertial dust collector in which the gas is made to spin so that particles, having more inertia than the gas, are thrown to the wall and fall out; effective on the medium fraction, poor on fine dust.

    used in [§4.4](#s4-4)

Electronic waste (e-waste)
:   Electrical and electronic equipment that has become obsolete through changes in fashion, style or status or through reaching the end of its useful life; it carries metals, plastics, lead, mercury and brominated flame retardants, so it needs dismantling and recovery rather than landfill.

    used in [§4.9](#s4-9)

Electrostatic precipitator
:   A particulate collector that charges dust in a strong electric field and deposits it on oppositely charged plates, later removed by rapping; it handles very large gas volumes at low pressure drop and the deck gives its efficiency as 99 per cent.

    used in [§4.4](#s4-4)

Eutrophication
:   Enrichment of a water body with nutrients, particularly nitrates and phosphates, that triggers excessive algal growth; the bloom dies, decomposers multiply, and the resulting oxygen demand kills fish and other aerobic organisms.

    used in [§4.8](#s4-8)

Fugitive emission
:   A non-point release that escapes from a large area rather than a discrete conveyance — tank and pool surfaces, and leaks from valves, taps and couplings; invisible to a stack test and often larger in total mass than the stack itself.

    used in [§4.1](#s4-1)

Integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC)
:   A power cycle in which coal is gasified to a synthesis gas, cleaned before combustion, burned in a gas turbine, and the turbine exhaust heat used to raise steam for a second turbine; the deck credits it with up to 45 per cent efficiency but only demonstration-scale operation.

    used in [§4.6](#s4-6)

Leachate
:   The liquid that percolates through a mass of waste, dissolving and carrying contaminants with it; in a sanitary landfill it is intercepted by the liner, collected by a drainage layer and piped to treatment so that it cannot reach groundwater.

    used in [§4.10](#s4-10)

Montreal Protocol
:   The 1987 international agreement that phased out ozone-depleting substances, beginning with chlorofluorocarbons and halons and then hydrochlorofluorocarbons; it is the reason the stratospheric chlorine burden is now falling.

    used in [§4.5](#s4-5)

Necrosis
:   Death of patches of plant tissue, seen as brown dead spots on a leaf; unlike chlorosis it is irreversible, because the cells are dead rather than merely depleted of pigment.

    used in [§4.3](#s4-3)

Ozone depletion
:   The gradual thinning of the stratospheric ozone layer caused by chlorine- and bromine-bearing compounds released by human activity; the layer absorbs 97 to 99 per cent of incoming ultraviolet, and about 5 per cent of it was lost between 1979 and 1990.

    used in [§4.5](#s4-5)

Ozone-depleting substance (ODS)
:   A compound that releases chlorine or bromine on exposure to strong ultraviolet light — chlorofluorocarbons, carbon tetrachloride and hydrochlorofluorocarbons for chlorine, halons, methyl bromide and hydrobromofluorocarbons for bromine.

    used in [§4.5](#s4-5)

Primary pollutant
:   A pollutant emitted directly from an identifiable source in an already harmful form — sulphur dioxide from a stack, particulate matter, oxides of nitrogen, volatile organic compounds, carbon monoxide or lead.

    used in [§4.1](#s4-1)

Secondary pollutant
:   A pollutant formed in the atmosphere by reaction between primary pollutants or between a primary pollutant and other constituents of the air; sulphuric acid mist and photochemical smog are the standard examples, and neither can be captured at a stack.

    used in [§4.1](#s4-1)

Waste hierarchy
:   The ranking of waste management options by preference — prevention, reuse, recycling, energy recovery, disposal — in which each step down consumes more energy and destroys more of the material's value than the step above.

    used in [§4.10](#s4-10)

#### Module 5 · Safe Working Environment, Welfare and PPE

4R concept
:   Recycling, renovation, recharge and reuse of wastewater, so that domestic sewage, industrial effluent and thermal or radioactive wastes are treated and returned to use rather than discharged — with sludge reused to generate cheaper fuel, gas and electricity.

    used in [§5.7](#s5-7)

Air-purifying respirator
:   A respirator that removes contaminants from the air it draws through a filter — particulate, chemical cartridge or gas mask, or powered (PAPR). It cannot be used in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, against a contaminant the cartridge is not rated for, or where the concentration is unknown.

    used in [§5.5](#s5-5)

Ambulance room
:   The equipped room a factory must provide under section 45 of the Factories Act, 1948 once the number of workers exceeds 500, so that a casualty can be held and stabilised on site rather than only dressed and sent away.

    used in [§5.3](#s5-3)

Atmosphere-supplying respirator
:   A respirator that provides clean, breathable air from its own source rather than filtering the surrounding atmosphere, either from a cylinder (self-contained breathing apparatus, SCBA) or through a supply line (supplied-air respirator, SAR).

    used in [§5.5](#s5-5)

First aid
:   The first and immediate assistance given to a person with a minor or serious illness or injury, to preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, or promote recovery until medical services arrive. It stabilises; it does not diagnose or treat.

    used in [§5.3](#s5-3)

Hard hat classes
:   Class G, general, protects against impact and penetration and is proof-tested to 2,200 volts; Class E, electrical, to 20,000 volts; Class C, conductive, gives bump protection only and no protection from falling objects or electrical hazards.

    used in [§5.5](#s5-5)

Hazard classification
:   The severity grading given to each inspection finding: A, major or high, requiring immediate action; B, serious or medium, requiring short-term action; C, minor or low, requiring long-term action. The levels are calibrated to the hazards of that workplace.

    used in [§5.2](#s5-2)

Hearing conservation programme
:   The structured programme — measurement, protection, audiometric testing, training and record-keeping — that makes a noise limit enforceable. Audiometry detects loss before the worker notices it, and training corrects the fit that the rating assumes.

    used in [§5.5](#s5-5)

Inspection checklist
:   A prepared list that clarifies inspection responsibilities, controls the activity and provides the record of what was examined. It is a prompt and a reporting device, never a boundary on what may be looked at.

    used in [§5.2](#s5-2)

Ion exchange
:   A reversible reaction in which an ion in solution is exchanged for a similarly charged ion attached to an immobile solid particle, either a naturally occurring inorganic zeolite or a synthetic organic resin. Used to remove dissolved ionic contaminants and to soften water.

    used in [§5.7](#s5-7)

Noise reduction rating
:   The single-number laboratory rating used to compare hearing protectors. Field attenuation is always lower than the rating, because a shallowly inserted plug or a muff broken by spectacle arms loses much of its protection.

    used in [§5.5](#s5-5)

Personal protective equipment
:   Equipment worn or held by the worker that protects against one or more hazards to health and safety, used only where the hazard cannot be eliminated or controlled at source. It is the last line of defence because it works only while it is worn, fitted and maintained.

    used in [§5.2](#s5-2), [§5.5](#s5-5)

Reverse osmosis
:   Purification of water through a semipermeable membrane. As a pre-treatment for deionisation it removes bacteria, organics and silica and reduces dissolved salt content by more than 95 per cent.

    used in [§5.7](#s5-7)

Safety inspection
:   The process of critically examining the workplace to identify and investigate hazards, confirm that standards are met, and initiate action that reduces the severity of what is found, so that the workplace is safe and free from unacceptable risk. It is an ongoing process, not a one-off certification.

    used in [§5.1](#s5-1)

Stabilisation of ecosystem
:   A method of controlling water pollution by working on the water body itself: reduction of waste at source, harvesting and removal of biomass, trapping of nutrients, and fish management. It restores the assimilative capacity that pollution has consumed.

    used in [§5.7](#s5-7)

Welfare facilities
:   The statutory provisions an employer must make for the ordinary physical needs of workers — washing, clothing storage, seating, first aid, canteen, shelter, rest and lunch rooms — under sections 42 to 47 of the Factories Act, 1948.

    used in [§5.4](#s5-4)
